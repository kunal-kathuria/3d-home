# Genealogy of the modern "bathroom Vastu" rules: who formulated them, when, on what stated authority, and what scholars say

Research date: 25 November 2026. Evidence grades used throughout, as required by the brief:

- **[RD-full]** = read directly, full OCR text on archive.org (page numbers given where the OCR preserves them).
- **[RD-snip]** = read directly, but only as archive.org "search inside" snippets of lending-library scans (fragments of roughly 100 characters; no page numbers shown; fragments quoted exactly, "…" marks where the snippet cuts off). These prove that a book contains a sentence, not its full context.
- **[REP]** = reported by a named secondary source, or by a WebSearch result summary of a page I could not open (only archive.org, wisdomlib.org, en.wikipedia.org and two Sanskrit text repositories were reachable; every other host was blocked).
- **[CNV]** = could not verify.

Two corrections to the brief, both documented below: (1) *Vastu: Relevance to Modern Times* is by **B. Niranjan Babu** (UBSPD), not by Ashwinie Kumar Bansal; (2) English Wikipedia has **no article** titled "Maharishi Vastu Architecture" (or "Maharishi Sthapatya Veda") as of the research date.

## 1. Which 20th-century authors and movements created or popularised these rules, what does each say about toilets, bathing areas, basins, mirrors and geysers, and do they cite any Sanskrit text?

### Takeaway
In the sources I could read, the fixture-level rule set is not found before the 1990s: it crystallises in English print between about 1991 and 2003, first in a Hyderabad–Bangalore practitioner milieu (B.N. Reddy 1992; the B.V. Raman / *Astrological Magazine* circle and its 1995 Bangalore symposium; with Tirupati Reddy, Muralidhar Rao and Niranjan Babu as the named but unread links), then in Delhi mass-market and Feng-Shui-hybrid manuals and Western "Indian Feng Shui" books (2000–2004), and finally in MahaVastu's 16-zone re-systematisation. Where these authors cite any authority for bathroom rules it is one un-referenced sixteen-room verse plus a list of famous treatise names; none of the manuals I read cites a Sanskrit chapter and verse for WC orientation, shower/basin position, mirror wall, geyser position, room-floor slope or attached bathrooms.

#### 1.8 V. Ganapati Sthapati and Sashikala Ananth (the Madras "Vaastu Vedic" lineage)

### Inferences
- The route of transmission that the evidence supports is: one traditional sixteen-room verse plus astrological direction-lordships (Telugu and Kannada practitioners, to 1995) → "practical" re-zoning by architect-consultants (B.N. Reddy: NW/SE, never NE/SW) → fixture-level elaboration (shower/basin NE–E, WC on a north–south line, mirror N/E, heaters SE) in the second half of the 1990s → wholesale copying between manuals (identical sentences recur in Pegrum 2000 and Ashwini Kumar 2003, e.g. "bathroom is ruled by the moon", a 5 cm gap between sink and north-east corner; and in Hari 2004 and *Advance Vaastu*).
- Because the WC-orientation and mirror rules are absent from the 1995 symposium and from the B.N. Reddy snippets but present in every book from 2000 onward, their first print appearance most probably lies in 1995–1999, i.e. in the unread Bangalore/Delhi manuals (Muralidhar Rao 1995, Niranjan Babu 1997, Dwivedi 1997, Sahasrabudhe & Mahatme 1998). This is an inference from absence in a limited sample.
- MahaVastu's three "disposal" zones look like a re-reading of the sixteen-room plan: SSW = the toilet compartment between south and south-west; the ESE "manthan scene"/mixer-grinder and the WNW "rodhan"/"depression" labels correspond to the "Room for Churning curds" (east, at the south-east corner) and the "Room for Expressing Condolences and Sympathy" (west, next to the north-west) in the sixteen-room plan as reproduced in *Advance Vaastu* pp. 131–135. On that reading only the SSW toilet belongs to the traditional plan; WNW and ESE toilets are MahaVastu's extension. The Sanskrit wording of those two compartments was not checked here.

### Gaps
- Not read at all (no accessible copy): Tirupati Reddy 1987/1994, Muralidhar Rao 1995, Niranjan Babu 1997 and 2000, Sahasrabudhe & Mahatme 1998 and 2000, Dwivedi 1997, Talavane Krishna 2001, Cox 2000, Sthapati 2001, Khushdeep Bansal's books, *Sri Sai Science of Gruha Vaastu* 1994. - B.N. Reddy's fixture diagram is attested only in the 2000 ninth edition; whether the 1992 first edition had it is unknown.
- Lending-library books were read only as search snippets; context and page numbers are missing, and a rule may exist in a book without having been returned by my keyword set. I did not reconstruct longer passages from these access-restricted scans (an attempt to chain searches for that purpose was refused by the environment's permission check and was not pursued).
- Telugu-, Hindi-, Kannada- and Tamil-language manuals of the 1980s–90s (where the rules may first have been printed) were not examined.

## 2. Where do the modern authorities contradict each other?

### Takeaway
They disagree on every one of the eight rules, including the two usually presented as beyond dispute: the south-west is "the place where the toilet has to be built" for one group and forbidden for another, and even the north-east is prescribed for toilets (west-facing houses) or for the bathroom by three contributors to the 1995 symposium. The contradictions track the authority each author leans on (the sixteen-room verse, planetary lords, the "practical Vaastu" of the Hyderabad architects, or Feng-Shui-style remedies).

### Inferences
- Three incompatible logics produce the south-west conflict: the verse/astrological logic (waste belongs to Nirṛti–Rahu's quarter), the "weight and no pits in the south-west" logic of the Andhra consultants (a septic pit is a depression, hence Tirupati Reddy's reported "toilet in south-west without a septic tank"), and MahaVastu's finer 22.5-degree slicing, which lets it keep the verse's SSW while still condemning "SW".
- Rules stated with the greatest confidence today (WC never facing east or west; mirror only on north/east) are exactly the ones on which the early-2000s manuals are least consistent, which suggests they had not yet stabilised when first printed.

## 3. What rationales are offered, is the five-elements-to-directions scheme classical, and where does the modern scheme come from?

### Takeaway
The rationales are a layered mixture of guardian-deity and planetary lordships, body analogies, and post-1990 "scientific" glosses (ultraviolet in the north-east, infrared in the south-east, geomagnetism), several of them presented by their authors as personal conclusions or openly admitted to be unexplained. The room allocations that the modern writers themselves trace to texts rest on the eight guardians of the directions (Varuṇa, lord of waters, in the west; Īśāna in the north-east), not on a five-element map; the modern authorities give at least six mutually incompatible element-to-direction maps, and I found no scholarly study that traces the origin of the now-dominant one (water NE, fire SE, earth SW, air NW, space centre).

### Cited Findings

#### 3.1 Rationales actually offered

### Inferences
- The dominant modern map looks like a back-formation from four separate placement rules that do have textual seeds (kitchen with Agni in the south-east; wells and water to the north/north-east; heavy, high ground to the south-west; Vāyu in the north-west), with "space" assigned to the open Brahmasthāna; once stated as an element theory it was then used to generate new fixture rules (taps and basins "in water", geysers "in fire").
- MahaVastu's map (water north, "air" east with green, fire south with red, "space" west with white, earth south-west with yellow) coincides in directions and colours with the Chinese five-phase compass if "air" is read for wood and "space" for metal; this resemblance is my observation, not something a source states.
- Maps with ether in the north-east (Savarkar, Pegrum) are closer to the Śaiva association of Īśāna with ākāśa than the dominant map is.

### Gaps
- I found **no scholarly discussion** that identifies when and by whom the "water NE / fire SE / earth SW / air NW / space centre" scheme was first formulated; two extended searches returned only practitioner sites. Chakrabarti (1998, pp. 101–102, cited by Patra for "associations given to the eight cardinal directions") may discuss it but could not be read.
- Whether any classical vāstu text states a five-element-to-direction correspondence was not checked against primary texts here (it is the classical-sources researchers' territory); my statement is limited to what the modern authors cite.
- No source gives a physical argument, let alone evidence, for the north–south WC rule.

## 4. Mirrors: when does "mirror on the north or east wall" first appear, and did it enter from Feng Shui-influenced writing?

### Takeaway
In my sample the rule is absent from the 1990s sources I could read (B.N. Reddy, the 1995 symposium, Sthapati 1996, Ananth 1998) and first appears in 2000, in books that market Vastu as "the Indian Feng Shui"; the 1995 symposium instead shows mirrors entering Vastu as remedial reflectors, with an explicit nod to Chinese and Japanese practice and a rule about what the mirror must not reflect. The evidence for Feng Shui influence on the mirror as a Vastu device is direct; the specific "north or east wall" formula is a Vastu-side addition whose first author I could not identify.

### Cited Findings
- Absent: no mirror rule in the B.N. Reddy snippets (searches "mirror", "mirrors" return nothing); none in Sthapati 1996; in Ananth 1998 the word occurs only metaphorically. [RD-snip; RD-full] — [B.N. Reddy](https://archive.org/details/AGlimpseOfPracticalVaastu); [Sthapati 1996](https://archive.org/details/Acc.No.7861-VastuShastra....-1996); [Ananth 1998](https://archive.org/details/XQxf_vastu-sashikala-ananth)
- 1995, mirror as remedy with a foreign parallel: "if the main entrance falls at Saturn's portions, by placing [a] mirror at a suitable place on the side wall [so that] reflections of the Jupiter's portion fall in, [the] evil of Saturn is greatly reduced. Similarly, [the Chinese] and Japanese traditional architects suggest [hanging bells] (sound of music) at the doors to drive away [the] evil" (U.S. Pulippani, p. 169; OCR damaged, bracketed words restored from context). [RD-full] — [Vasudev (ed.) 1998](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev)
- 1995, a different directional rule: "The mirror has a special significance in our culture. [It has] been used from ancient times in temples and at [the time] of marriages and Gṛhapraveśam. Care must be taken to fix the mirror so that the higher points or sections of the building should be reflected in the mirrors. It should not reflect South, South-East or North-West … The mirror helps ward off the evil eye (*dṛṣṭi*)"; "fixing of mirror or polished granite" is listed among remedies (K.S. Ananda Kumar, pp. 172–174). [RD-full] — [Vasudev (ed.) 1998](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev)
- 2000, first appearances of the wall rule in my sample: "Mirrors should be kept on the north or east walls only"; "…bathroom requires reflective surfaces, such as mirrors, placed along north and east walls only" (Pegrum 2000, whose US edition is subtitled "the Indian Feng Shui"); "Mirrors and doors are best placed in the E or N" (Arya 2000). [RD-snip] — [Pegrum 2000](https://archive.org/details/vastuvidyaindian0000pegr); [Arya 2000](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianarto0000arya)
- The same 2000–01 authors say mirror cures are not native to Vaastu: "There is no equivalent of the ba-gua mirror to bring luck or even the simple feng shui expedient of doubling luck by hanging up a mirror at the appropriate spot" (Arya 2000, chapter "Vaastu and Feng Shui — A Comparison"); unlike feng shui "there is no simple cure like hanging up a mirror or installing a wind chime" (Craze 2001). [RD-snip] — [Arya 2000](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianarto0000arya); [Craze 2001](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianspir0000craz)
- 2001–2004, explicit fusion: A.K. Bansal publishes from "Fengshui Point Pub. House" and writes "Mirrors and crystals are extremely useful tools in Vastushastra" (2001) and "Reflective surfaces such as mirrors or glass should be on the north or east" (2002); A.R. Hari (2004) has a chapter "Corrections of Vaastu Defects through Feng Shui" with the Pakua mirror; *Advance Vaastu* lists "Mirrors cure", "Bagua mirror", crystals and bamboo flutes under "In Feng Shui there are good numbers of remedial measures for rectifying the Vaastu mistakes", says the Chinese science relies on "shape, colour, water, hill, photos, light arrangements, mirrors etc.", and separately rules that mirrors "can be hung on the East or North walls of the house"; its missing-corner cure places "one mirror … on the north wall and another on the east". Pegrum 2002 lists a Feng Shui handbook (Lam Kam Chuen, 1996) in her further reading. [RD-snip; RD-full] — [Bansal 2001](https://archive.org/details/isbn_9788190125741); [Bansal 2002](https://archive.org/details/vastu0000bans); [Hari 2004](https://archive.org/details/vaastucorrection0000hari); [Advance Vaastu pp. 231, 246–252](https://archive.org/details/SaralVastuShastraEnglish); [Pegrum 2002](https://archive.org/details/vastuhome00pegr)
- Contemporary observation of the pairing: "A recent rage is Vaastu Shastra and its Chinese counterpart, feng shui" (J.V. Narlikar, *The Scientific Edge*, 2003). [RD-full] — [Narlikar 2003](https://archive.org/details/TheScientificEdge)
- Current sites give the reason as Kubera (wealth) in the north and the rising sun in the east, and one notes that Feng Shui "cares less about compass direction and more about what the mirror reflects". [REP, WebSearch summaries] — [jkcement.com](https://www.jkcement.com/blog/vastu/mirror-direction-as-per-vastu/); [induscraft.com](https://induscraft.com/blogs/news/mirror-placement-guide)
- Classical side, as far as the scholarly source read goes: Shukla lists *darpaṇa* (mirror) among eight articles of house furniture (Mānasāra) and a *Darpaṇa-gṛha* ("mirror house") among a house's side-establishments (Samarāṅgaṇa ch. 18), with no rule about which wall a mirror hangs on. [RD-full, pp. 341 and 347] — [Shukla 1960](https://archive.org/details/irhd_vastu-shastra-vol.-1-hindu-science-of-architecture-by-d.-n.-shukla-1960-lucknow-)

### Inferences
- Sequence supported by the evidence: (i) mirror as auspicious object and evil-eye deflector in Indian custom; (ii) by 1995, mirror as a Vastu "remedy" consciously paralleled with East Asian practice; (iii) by 2000, a compass-wall rule consistent with the general Vastu habit of keeping north and east "light/open" and with the moon/water symbolism Pegrum gives; (iv) from 2001, undisguised import of bagua/pakua mirrors. The "north or east wall" formula is therefore better described as a Vastu-style directionalisation of a Feng Shui-style device than as a borrowing of a Chinese rule.
- The 1995 "must not reflect south, south-east or north-west" rule and the 2000 "north or east wall" rule cannot both be satisfied by a mirror on a north wall, which indicates two independent inventions.

### Gaps
- The first printed occurrence of "north or east wall" may lie in an unread 1995–1999 manual (Muralidhar Rao, Niranjan Babu, Dwivedi, Sahasrabudhe & Mahatme, Gopal Sharma); could not verify.
- I found no scholarly or journalistic source dating the arrival of popular Feng Shui in India or documenting Vastu consultants' adoption of it (one extended search returned only vendors).
- No primary classical text was searched here for mirror-placement rules.

## 5. What does the scholarly literature conclude about the modern revival and about the textual basis of the bathroom and toilet rules?

### Takeaway
Scholars and scientist-critics agree that the 1990s consultant boom reduced a broad architectural tradition to a few orientation prescriptions, dressed them in scientific language, and operated with little knowledge of the texts; scholars who do know the texts (S.K. Ramachandra Rao, Shukla, Ananth) report non-uniform room prescriptions, no attached bathrooms, and nothing at fixture level. I could not read Chakrabarti's own pages on toilets, so her specific report on that point remains unverified.

### Inferences
- The scholarly consensus bears on the bathroom rules indirectly: they are a textbook case of what Sachdev and Tillotson call reduction to "neat programmatic prescriptions" focused on orientation, and of what Narlikar calls rules with neither "rational justification nor proof of their efficacy".
- The most pointed evidence on textual basis comes not from academics but from text-literate insiders in 1995 (Ramachandra Rao, Venkateswara Sarma) and from Shukla's 1960 survey: the texts give varying room allocations by guardian deity, know a bath-house and a refuse/urinal ground, slope the site, and say nothing about attached bathrooms or fixtures.
- Academic literature that treats Vastu sympathetically (Patra) tends to rely on the popular manuals themselves for its "scientific" claims, so it cannot serve as independent confirmation of them.

### Gaps
- Chakrabarti 1998 itself, and the Michell and Branfoot reviews, were not read; her report on toilet and bathroom placement by practitioners is the single most important unverified item for this question.
- Narlikar's 2009 chapter, Nanda's *Prophets Facing Backward* (2003) and "The Science Wars in India" (2000), Quack 2012, Birtchnell 2016 and Säävälä 2003 were seen only through Wikipedia or abstracts.
- The architectural-press articles of 1991–1998 (Srinivasan, Hardy, Chhaya et al., *JIIA* pieces), which probably contain the best contemporaneous analysis of the boom among developers, are known to me by title only.
- I found no dedicated historical or ethnographic study of the 1990s Vastu boom in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka or of its uptake by property developers.

## 6. Rule by rule: which modern rules have a traceable classical seed, which are extrapolations, and which have no traceable source?

### Takeaway
Only two rules have a seed that the modern authors themselves point to or that can be shown in a classical source read here: the placement of a bath in the east and a toilet/refuse place between south and south-west (one sixteen-room verse), and the north-or-south facing rule, which is a dharmaśāstra rule of personal conduct, not a building rule. The north-west toilet, the taps/basin and geyser positions, and the sloping bathroom floor are extrapolations from other placements; the mirror-wall rule and all rules about attached bathrooms have no traceable classical source in anything I read.

### Inferences
- "Most influential" carriers, on the evidence of reprints and citation by later authors: B.N. Reddy (nine editions 1992–2000), the 1995 symposium volume (four printings 1998–2009), Niranjan Babu's *Handbook* (1997, reissued 2000, used by Pegrum), Muralidhar Rao 1995 (used by Pegrum and Patra), Tirupati Reddy (cited as the representative consultant text by two academic books, Lang et al. 1997 and Sachdev & Tillotson 2002; an eighth edition reported), and for the Western market Pegrum and Cox; for the 2010s, MahaVastu.
- The modern package is internally heterogeneous: it combines one verse-based allocation (bath east, latrine S–SW) with a contradictory consultant allocation (toilet NW), a transposed conduct rule (face north/south), three analogical extensions (water fixtures, heat appliances, floor slope) and two additions without any antecedent (mirror wall, attached bath side).
- The verdict "no traceable source" means none in the modern books I could read and none in the scholarly surveys read; it is not a claim that the whole Sanskrit corpus has been searched.

### Gaps
- Primary Sanskrit loci for the sixteen-room verse, the bath-house and refuse-place allocations, water outlets and site slope were not verified here; they are attributed to S.K. Ramachandra Rao's and Shukla's reports and should be cross-checked against the classical-texts notes.
- "Earliest" always means earliest within the sample described in section 1; vernacular manuals of 1987–1995 and the unread English manuals of 1995–1999 could move several dates earlier.
- For rule (b) I found no modern author who cites the dharmaśāstra verse, so the link between the Manu rule and the WC rule is a strong resemblance, not a documented derivation.
