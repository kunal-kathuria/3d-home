## 1. Facing direction when relieving oneself: what the texts say, whether they agree, the reasons given, and whether modern Vastu keeps the day/night rule

### Takeaway

The majority rule in the Dharmasūtras, Smṛtis and Purāṇas is "face north by day and at twilight, face south at night", combined with a separate ban on facing or looking at the sun, moon, fire, wind, water, cows, Brahmins and gods, and with an explicit exemption in shade, darkness, danger or illness; it is a rule about a person's posture in the open, and the texts give only "life is not harmed" and "intellect perishes" as reasons. The texts do not agree with one another (Āpastamba's quoted verse assigns south to defecation and north to urination; a Yama verse cited by Aparārka prescribes facing west in the forenoon and east in the afternoon), no text read forbids east or west as compass points in the way modern Vastu does (the one possible exception is a tentatively read half-verse, Vāmana Purāṇa 14.30), and the modern "seat on a north-south axis, never east or west" formula drops the day/night alternation and all the exemptions.

#### 1a. Manusmṛti 4.45–52 (Sanskrit, Bühler, Jha)

- **Manu 4.45** [RD, Sanskrit]: *nānnam adyād ekavāsā na nagnaḥ snānam ācaret | na mūtraṃ pathi kurvīta na bhasmani na govraje*. Bühler [RD]: "Let him not eat, dressed with one garment only; let him not bathe naked; let him not void urine on a road, on ashes, or in a cow-pen". Medhātithi [RD, Jha tr.]: "'Urine' here stands for excretions of all kinds." — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft); [Jha/Medhātithi 4.45](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200149.html)
- **Manu 4.46–47** [RD]: *na phālakṛṣṭe na jale na cityāṃ na ca parvate | na jīrṇadevāyatane na valmīke kadācana || na sasattveṣu garteṣu na gacchann api na sthitaḥ | na nadītīram āsādya na ca parvatamastake*. Bühler [RD]: "Nor on ploughed land, in water, on an altar of bricks, on a mountain, on the ruins of a temple, nor ever on an ant-hill, Nor in holes inhabited by living creatures, nor while he walks or stands, nor on reaching the bank of a river, nor on the top of a mountain." Every place named is outdoors. — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft)
- **Manu 4.48** [RD]: *vāyvagnivipram ādityam apaḥ paśyaṃs tathaiva gāḥ | na kadācana kurvīta viṇmūtrasya visarjanam*. Bühler [RD]: "Let him never void faeces or urine, facing the wind, or a fire, or looking towards a Brahmana, the sun, water, or cows." Medhātithi [RD, Jha tr.]: "What is meant is that one shall not do the act facing the wind; and the other things he shall not look at, while urinating, even by turning his body towards them." — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft); [Jha/Medhātithi 4.48](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200152.html)
- **Manu 4.49** [RD]: *tiraskṛtyoccaret kāṣṭhaloṣṭhapatratṛṇādinā | niyamya prayato vācaṃ saṃvītāṅgo 'vaguṇṭhitaḥ*. Bühler [RD]: "He may ease himself, having covered (the ground) with sticks, clods, leaves, grass, and the like, restraining his speech, (keeping himself) pure, wrapping up his body, and covering his head." The verse presupposes bare ground that the person covers himself. — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft)
- **Manu 4.50 (the day/night verse)** [RD]: *mūtroccārasamutsargaṃ divā kuryād udaṅmukhaḥ | dakṣiṇābhimukho rātrau saṃdhyayoś ca yathā divā*. Bühler [RD]: "Let him void faeces and urine, in the daytime turning to the north, at night turning towards the south, during the two twilights in the same (position) as by day." Jha [RD]: "He shall do the passing of urine and faeces during the day, with his face towards the north; and at night, with face towards the south; and at the two twilights as during the day." Medhātithi's whole comment on this verse [RD, Jha tr.] is one gloss: "'Samutsarga'—passing." He gives no reason here. — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft); [Jha/Medhātithi, verse numbered 4.51](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200155.html)
- **Manu 4.51 (the exemption verse)** [RD]: *chāyāyām andhakāre vā rātrāv ahani vā dvijaḥ | yathāsukhamukhaḥ kuryāt prāṇabādhabhayeṣu ca*. Bühler [RD]: "In the shade or in darkness a Brahmana may, both by day and at night, do it, assuming any position he pleases; likewise when his life is in danger." Jha [RD]: "In the shade, or in darkness, the Brāhmaṇa may—during the day or the night—do it, with his face towards any direction he pleases; as also where there is danger to life, and when there is fear." — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 137](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft); [Jha/Medhātithi, verse numbered 4.52](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200156.html)
- **Medhātithi on the exemption verse** [RD, Jha tr.]: "'Shade'—i.e., where the sun's rays are shut out by walls or doors, etc. 'Darkness'—the obstruction of light by clouds or fogs or eclipses or by night. … He shall pass urine with his face towards that direction which he finds convenient. This rule pertains to such 'darkness' as makes it impossible for the directions to be determined. 'Danger to life,' and 'fear'—due to thieves, etc." — [Jha/Medhātithi, verse numbered 4.52](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200156.html)
- **Two schools on how wide the exemption is** [REP: Jha's explanatory note summarising the Vīramitrodaya, Āhnika p. 30]: (a) Vīramitrodaya, "with the support of Kalpataru": "This verse supplies an exception to the law regarding the facing of the North or the East etc.; so that this latter law remains applicable to the day, when there is light, and also to the night when there is moon-light." (b) "The author of Smṛticandrikā, Mādhavācārya, Kullūka Bhaṭṭa and others have held the view that the first half applies to cases where one has lost all sense of direction; but this view has been rejected [by the Vīramitrodaya] on the ground that there is no authority for restricting the rule in this manner." Madanapārijāta (p. 42) and Vidhānapārijāta agree with (b) (fog, loss of direction). — [Jha, notes on verse numbered 4.52](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200156.html)
- **Manu 4.52 (the "intellect perishes" verse)** [RD]: *pratyagniṃ pratisūryaṃ ca pratisomodakadvijam | pratigu prativātaṃ ca prajñā naśyati mehataḥ* (Kullūka reads *pratigāṃ*). Bühler [RD]: "The intellect of (a man) who voids urine against a fire, the sun, the moon, in water, against a Brahmana, a cow, or the wind, perishes." Bühler's note [RD]: "Medh. places verse 52 immediately after this [48]." — [GRETIL Manusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, SBE 25, pp. 136–137](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft)
- **Medhātithi sees the tension between "face north" and "do not face the sun"** [RD, Jha tr., on the verse Jha numbers 4.49 = Bühler 4.52]: "'Since it has been laid down that one should pass urine, facing the north,—and the sun rises in the east,—how can one ever face the sun, in view of which possibility we have the present prohibition?' The present verse is a commendatory supplement … Then again, during the northern solstice, the sun moving towards the north, it would be possible to face the sun. Or, the prohibition may be taken as meant for the common people (who may not know the rule regarding urinating with face towards the north)." He also rejects a variant reading *pratisandhyam* ("facing the twilight") because "during the two twilights it shall be done as during the day". — [Jha/Medhātithi, verse numbered 4.49](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200153.html)
- **Status of 4.52** [REP: Jha's note]: Medhātithi "remarks that 'some people do not read this verse in the present Discourse'", and Jha adds that "this verse is not quoted in any of the important Nibandhas". — [Jha, notes on verse numbered 4.49](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200153.html)
- **Mahābhārata parallel** [REP: Jha quoting Burnell]: the day/night verse "occurs in the Mahābhārata 13.104.76, following the one that is equivalent to Manu 52, but with the var. lec. (a) *ubhe mūtrapurīṣe tu* (b) (in the second pāda) *tathā hy āyur na riṣyate*" ("for thus life is not harmed"). Not read directly in the Mahābhārata. — [Jha, notes on verse numbered 4.51](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200155.html)

#### 1c. Viṣṇu Smṛti and Yājñavalkya Smṛti with the Mitākṣarā

- **Viṣṇu Smṛti 60.1–2** [RD]: *brāhme muhūrte utthāya mūtrapurīṣotsargaṃ kuryāt | dakṣiṇābhimukho rātrau divā codaṅmukhaḥ saṃdhyayoś ca*. Jolly [RD]: "In (the last watch of the night, which is called) the hour sacred to Brahman, let him rise and void his excrements. By night (let him void them) facing the south, by day and during either twilight (let him void them) facing the north." — [GRETIL Viṣṇusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_viSNusmRti.htm); [Jolly, SBE 7, pp. 194–195](https://archive.org/details/institutesofvish00joll)
- **Viṣṇu Smṛti 60.3–23** [RD]: a list of forbidden places, all outdoors: uncovered ground (60.3), ploughed field (4), shade (5, *na chāyāyām*; Jolly supplies in his translation "of a tree (fit to be used for sacrifices)", words that are not in the Sanskrit), barren soil, fresh grass, where there are insects, a ditch, an ant-hill, a path, a public road, a place defiled by another, a garden, near a garden or water, ashes, coal, cow-dung, a cattle-fold, "in the air", water; then 60.22 *na pratyanilānalendvarkastrīgurubrāhmaṇānām*, Jolly: "Nor facing the wind, or fire, or the moon, or the sun, or a woman, or a (father or other) Guru, or a Brahmana"; 60.23 "Nor without having enveloped his head". — [GRETIL Viṣṇusmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_viSNusmRti.htm); [Jolly, SBE 7, pp. 195–196](https://archive.org/details/institutesofvish00joll)
- **Yājñavalkya 1.16** [RD]: *divāsaṃdhyāsu karṇasthabrahmasūtra udaṅmukhaḥ | kuryān mūtrapurīṣe ca rātrau ced dakṣiṇāmukhaḥ*. Jha's rendering [RD]: "During the day, and at the twilights one shall perform urination and stooling with the sacred thread resting on his ears—facing the north; but at night, facing the south." — [GRETIL Yājñavalkyasmṛti](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_yAjJavalkyasmRti.htm); [Jha, comparative notes at Manu 4.49–50](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200154.html)
- **Mitākṣarā (Vijñāneśvara) on Yājñavalkya 1.16** [RD, Sanskrit; my gloss]: *karṇasthaṃ brahmasūtraṃ yasya sa tathoktaḥ | karṇaś ca dakṣiṇaḥ … asāv ahani saṃdhyayoś ca udaṅmukho mūtrapurīṣe kuryāt | cakārād bhasmādirahite deśe | rātrau tu dakṣiṇāmukhaḥ* — "… by day and at the twilights he should void urine and faeces facing north; the word 'and' implies 'in a place free of ashes and the like'; at night, facing south." ### Inferences

- The real textual root of the modern "north-south seat, never east or west" claim is the Smṛti conduct rule *divā udaṅmukhaḥ, rātrau dakṣiṇāmukhaḥ* (Manu 4.50 and its parallels), fused with the separate ban on facing the sun and moon (Manu 4.48, 4.52). North and south are the two directions the default rule names, and east and west are where the sun and moon rise and set; Medhātithi himself reasons that way when he asks how a north-facing man could ever face the sun, "the sun rises in the east".
- No text read states an east/west prohibition in compass terms, with the uncertain exception of Vāmana Purāṇa 14.30d. The stated prohibitions are object-relative (sun, moon, fire, wind, water, cows, Brahmins, gods, women, guru, stars, twilight). Two authorities positively use the east–west axis: Yama (west in the forenoon, east in the afternoon, i.e. back to the sun) and Āpastamba's verse (eat facing east, wash feet facing west). On Yama's rule a fixed north-south seat is not required and a fixed west-facing seat would be correct every forenoon.
- The modern rule is not the classical rule. Classical: a person turns north in daylight and south at night. Modern: a fixture is aligned once, so that the user faces the same way at all hours. A seat that makes the user face north obeys Manu 4.50 by day and breaks it at night, and a seat facing south does the reverse. The day/night alternation, which every majority-rule text states, cannot be satisfied by any fixed seat.
- On the texts' own terms an enclosed room is the exempt case. Manu 4.51 and Vasiṣṭha 6.13 release the direction rule "in the shade or in darkness"; Medhātithi glosses shade as "where the sun's rays are shut out by walls or doors", Bühler glosses Vasiṣṭha's shade as that "of houses", and the Kalpataru and Vīramitrodaya confine the direction rule to open daylight and moonlit night. Only the narrower reading of Kullūka, the Smṛticandrikā and Mādhava (exempt only when directions cannot be told) would leave a roofed toilet inside the rule. This is my inference from the commentators; no commentator read discusses a latrine room.
- The reasons the texts give are religious and moral (reverence for luminaries, fire, cows, Brahmins and gods; loss of intellect; shortened life). None of the texts or commentators read gives a physical mechanism such as magnetism or "energy flow"; those belong to the modern pages.

## 2. Place and distance: where one should relieve oneself relative to the house, and whether any text contemplates a latrine inside the house

### Takeaway

Every Dharmaśāstra and Purāṇa passage read sends the householder out of and away from the dwelling: "far from his house, towards the south or south-west" (Āpastamba 1.31.2), "nor close to his dwelling" (Gautama 9.39), "far from his dwelling" (Manu 4.151, Yājñavalkya 1.154), and "to the south-west, beyond an arrow's flight" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.11.9; Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa as quoted in the Smṛticandrikā). None of these texts describes or even mentions a latrine inside a dwelling; the texts that do treat a latrine as part of a building are of a different kind (Arthaśāstra, Caraka's sick-house, the Buddhist Vinaya), and they give it no compass direction (sections 4 and 6).

### Cited Findings

- **Āpastamba 1.11.31.2** [RD]: *ārāc cāvasathān mūtrapurīṣe kuryād dakṣiṇāṃ diśaṃ dakṣiṇāparāṃ vā*. Bühler [RD]: "He shall void excrements far from his house, having gone towards the south or south-west." Jha's rendering [RD]: "Far away from the dwelling-house, to the south or to the south-west of it, he shall perform urination and the evacuating of the bowels" (Jha cites it as 1.37.2, evidently a misprint for 1.31.2). No distance measure is given. — [GRETIL Āpastamba Dharmasūtra](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_ApastambadharmasUtra.htm); [Bühler, SBE 2, p. 94](https://archive.org/details/sacredlawsofarya00buhliala); [Jha, comparative notes at Manu 4.151](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200259.html)
- **Āpastamba 1.11.31.3 (the night rule)** [RD]: *astamite ca bahir grāmād ārād āvasathād vā mūtrapurīṣayoḥ karma varjayet*. Bühler [RD]: "But after sunset he must not void excrements outside the village or far from his house." ### Inferences

- The combination "south or south-west" + "an arrow's flight" + "far from the dwelling" is a composite of Āpastamba (direction, no distance), the Viṣṇu and Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇas (south-west and arrow's flight) and Manu/Yājñavalkya (far, no direction). No single text read gives all three, and Manu gives no direction at all.
- The south-west (*nairṛtī*) placement in these texts is a direction of travel away from the house for open-air relief. It is not a statement about where a room should sit in a plan. Any modern reading that turns it into "the toilet room belongs in the south-west" (or any other sector) is transferring the rule to a different object.
- Three details show that excreta near or in the house were a practical fact even for the dharma authors: the night rule (Āpastamba 1.31.3), the instruction to throw faeces and urine "out of the house" (Mārkaṇḍeya 35.31; Yājñavalkya 1.154 with the Mitākṣarā) and Bühler's "remove urine" for Manu 4.151. They imply vessels or night use near home, though no text read says so explicitly.
- Kullūka's gloss of *āvasatha* as the fire-room shows that the governing concern is ritual purity of the fires and the dwelling, the same concern behind the bans on facing fire, sun and Brahmins.

### Gaps

- Hārīta, Devala, Yama and Śaṅkha place-rules were seen only in Jha's English summaries of the digests.
- Could not verify the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa arrow-flight verse in a primary text.
- Could not verify what the 1916 JBORS article cited by Mackay says, or which Caraka passage it relied on for "latrines for the sick and infirm only" (Caraka Sū 15.6, read here, does provide a latrine in the house built for a patient under treatment, which fits the claim).
- No gṛhyasūtra was read directly for this question.

## 3. Bathing: where to bathe and which way to face in Dharmaśāstra and Āyurveda, and whether any rule ties the bath to the east or north-east

### Takeaway

The dharma texts place the daily bath outdoors in natural water (rivers, god-dug ponds, lakes, springs) and allow a bath at home with drawn water only as a fallback; the Āyurvedic texts discuss the benefits, water temperature and contraindications of bathing and say nothing about a bathing place's direction. No Dharmaśāstra or Āyurveda passage read, and no Purāṇic conduct chapter, ties the bath to the east or north-east: the only directional elements there are a time cue (bathe when the east reddens), facing the sun when plunging, and facing east or north for the sipping and cleansing rites. The one exception found is not a conduct rule but a house-planning list embedded in the astrology chapter of the Nārada Purāṇa (1.56.585), which puts the bath-house (*snānāgāra*) in the east.

### Inferences

- In the dharma texts bathing is a rite performed at open water, so a "bathroom direction" question does not arise for them; the domestic bath is a concession (courtyard, drawn water, hot water for the sick) with reduced ritual merit.
- The only "bath in the east" statement found outside the vāstu manuals is the room list in Nārada Purāṇa 1.56.585, which sits in that Purāṇa's astrology chapter and repeats the manuals' eight-direction scheme. It is evidence that the scheme circulated in Purāṇic compilations, but it is a planning list for a *snānāgāra*, with no reason given, and the parallel Agni Purāṇa list fills the east with a different room. It does not come from the conduct (ācāra) tradition that governs how a person bathes.
- The association of water and ritual with the north-east in the Arthaśāstra (2.4.8: sacrifice-and-water place next to the priest's quarters) may be an early relative of the later vāstu preference for water in the north-east, but the text applies it to a city precinct and to drinking or ritual water, not to a place for washing the body. This is an inference; the Arthaśāstra itself does not connect it with bathing.
- Āyurveda treats bathing and excretion as matters of health and decorum. Where it mentions a latrine and bath-room in a building (Caraka Sū 15.6) it treats them as equipment a competent builder provides, alongside the mortar and the kitchen.

### Gaps

- Gṛhyasūtra bathing rules (for example the *snātaka* bath) and the Snānasūtra of Kātyāyana were not read directly, so a facing rule there (other than "facing the sun", reported by Kane) cannot be excluded.
- The Sanskrit of Caraka Sū 5, 8 and 15 and of Suśruta Ci 24 was not opened; the terms behind "latrine" and "bath room" in Caraka Sū 15.6 could not be verified in the original.
- Yājñavalkya 1.159 and Dakṣa on bathing were taken from Kane's summary only.
- The Mānasollāsa and other medieval court texts on the royal bath were not consulted.
- The Nārada Purāṇa room list was read only in the GRETIL e-text and glossed by me; its date, its source and any published translation were not checked. The Matsya Purāṇa's house-building chapters are outside the portion of that Purāṇa available on GRETIL (chapters 1–176) and were not read; the Purāṇic vāstu chapters in general overlap with the vāstu-manual researcher's scope.

## 4. Arthaśāstra: latrine pit, drain, well and their set-backs (3.8), the royal residence (1.20), city sanitation (2.36), and whether any of these facilities is assigned to a compass direction

### Takeaway

The Arthaśāstra is the clearest classical witness that latrines, rubbish pits, drains and bath-rooms were ordinary parts of buildings: it lists a latrine and a bath-room among the fittings of royal store buildings (2.5.6), treats the dung-hill or cesspit (*avaskara*), waste-water drain (*bhrama*), well and latrine (*varcas*) as features of urban houses regulated by set-backs, nuisance fines and shared-use rights (3.8), and fines defecation and urination in public places (2.36.28). It regulates them by distance from the neighbour's wall and by nuisance, never by compass direction; where the same work does zone by direction (the royal city in 2.4) it names the kitchen, stores, stables and "places for sacrifices and for water" but no latrine, drain or bath.

#### 4c. Book 1, chapter 20 (the royal residence) and 1.21

- **1.20.1–2, concealed passages** [RD]: *vāstukapraśaste deśe saprākāraparikhādvāram anekakakṣyāparigatam antaḥpuraṃ kārayet | kośagṛhavidhānena madhye vāsagṛham, gūḍhabhittisaṃcāraṃ mohanagṛhaṃ tanmadhye vā vāsagṛham, bhūmigṛhaṃ vā … anekasuruṅgāsaṃcāraṃ …*. ### Inferences

- For the Arthaśāstra a latrine is a nuisance to be managed between neighbours, like a drain or a ladder, and an amenity to be provided in public buildings, like a well. It is a civic and legal matter. The work shows no trace of the idea that a latrine or bath has an auspicious or inauspicious compass sector.
- The Arthaśāstra and the Dharmaśāstra describe different settings. The dharma rules assume a man walking out to open ground; the Arthaśāstra assumes dense urban housing with party walls, where excreta are disposed of on the plot. The facing-direction rule belongs to the first setting and is not repeated in the second.
- Popular citations of "Arthaśāstra 3.8" as proof that every house had to have a latrine rest on Shamasastry's tentative translation; on Kangle's text the sentence restricts siting, and the word in it is *avaskara* (dung-hill or refuse pit), while the word for latrine (*varcas*) occurs in 2.5.6 and 3.8.28.

## 5. Kāmasūtra 1.4 on the townsman's house: anything on bathing or latrine placement?

### Takeaway

Kāmasūtra 1.4 describes the townsman's house only as "near water", with a garden, separate work areas and two bedrooms, and then lists a daily bath among his routines; it names no bath-room and no latrine and gives no direction for anything.

### Cited Findings

- **1.4.3, the house** [RD, Sanskrit; my gloss]: *tatra bhavanam āsannodakaṃ vṛkṣavāṭikavad vibhaktakarmakakṣaṃ dvivāsagṛhaṃ kārayet* — "There he should have a house built that has water close by, a tree-garden, separate quarters for different activities, and two bedrooms." — [GRETIL Kāmasūtra](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_vAtsyAyana-kAmasUtra.htm)
- **1.4.4, the furnishing (*bhavananyāsa*)** [RD, Sanskrit; my summary]: the outer bedroom has a soft bed with pillows and a white cover, a couch, at its head a stand and a ledge holding the night's unguents, garlands, a wax pot, perfume, citron peel and betel; "a spittoon on the floor" (*bhūmau patadgrahaḥ*); a lute hung on a peg, a painting board and box of brushes, a book, garlands; a round seat, gaming boards; outside, cages of pet birds; in a secluded spot a place for woodworking and other pastimes; in the garden a shaded, cushioned swing and a flower-strewn bench. No washing place, privy or drain is mentioned. — [GRETIL Kāmasūtra](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_vAtsyAyana-kAmasUtra.htm)
- **1.4.5, the only allusion to relieving oneself** [RD, Sanskrit; my gloss]: *sa prātar utthāya kṛtaniyatakṛtyaḥ gṛhītadantadhāvanaḥ …* — "rising in the morning, having performed the fixed necessary acts, having cleaned his teeth …". Where this is done is not said. — [GRETIL Kāmasūtra](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_vAtsyAyana-kAmasUtra.htm)
- **1.4.6, bathing schedule** [RD, Sanskrit; my gloss]: *nityaṃ snānam | dvitīyakam utsādanam | tṛtīyakaḥ phenakaḥ | caturthakam āyuṣyam | pañcamakaṃ daśamakaṃ vā pratyāyuṣyam ity ahīnam | sātatyāc ca saṃvṛtakakṣāsvedāpanodaḥ* — "a bath daily; a rub-down every second day; a foaming agent every third; shaving every fourth; the other [body hair] every fifth or tenth, without fail; and constant removal of sweat from the armpits." — [GRETIL Kāmasūtra](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_vAtsyAyana-kAmasUtra.htm)

### Inferences

- "Near water" (*āsannodaka*) and the daily bath suggest bathing at a nearby tank, river or well, in line with the dharma texts' preference for natural water, though the text does not say so.
- The silence of the most detailed classical description of a well-to-do urban house on any bath-room or latrine, while it bothers to mention the spittoon and the bird cages, fits the picture that these were not planned rooms of an ordinary house in the author's world. This is an argument from silence.

### Gaps

- No published translation (Burton; Doniger and Kakar) and no commentary (Yaśodhara's Jayamaṅgalā) was read for 1.4; the glosses above are mine. Could not verify whether Yaśodhara explains *vibhaktakarmakakṣa* or *kṛtaniyatakṛtya* as including a privy or bathing place.

## 6. Buddhist Vinaya (Cullavagga) rules on monastery privies (*vaccakuṭi*) and bath-houses (*jantāghara*): any directional rules?

### Takeaway

The Pali Vinaya authorises built urinals, cesspits, privy huts and hot bath-houses inside the monastery grounds and specifies their construction and etiquette in detail (raised plinth, brick, stone or wood lining, a floor slab with a hole, foot-rests, lids, doors, drains, water jars), but the passages read contain no compass direction at all: the only locational rule is "at one side" of the monastery.

### Cited Findings

- **Cullavagga V.35.1 (Vin II 140–141), urinals** [RD, Pali and Horner]: *Tena kho pana samayena bhikkhū ārāme tahaṃ tahaṃ passāvaṃ karonti. Ārāmo dussati. … "Anujānāmi bhikkhave, ekamantaṃ passāvaṃ kātun"ti*. Horner: "Now at that time monks relieved themselves here, there and everywhere in a monastery; the monastery was soiled. … 'I allow you, monks, to relieve yourselves at one side.' The monastery became nasty smelling. 'I allow you, monks, a vessel.' Sitting down, it was painful. 'I allow, monks, urinal shoes.' The urinal shoes were public; monks were ashamed to relieve themselves. 'I allow, monks, three (kinds of) fences to fence them in: a fence of bricks, a fence of stones, a fence of wood.' The vessel being uncovered became nasty smelling. 'I allow, monks, a lid.'" — [GRETIL Cullavagga (Pali)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/2_pali/1_tipit/1_vin/cullav_u.htm); [Horner, Book of the Discipline vol. 5, p. 196](https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.718)
- **Cullavagga V.35.2–4 (Vin II 141–142), cesspit and privy hut** [RD, Pali and Horner]: the same sequence for defecation: *vaccakūpa* ("a cesspool"), lined with "a piling of bricks, a piling of stones, a piling of wood", raised because it "was too low to the ground" and flooded, with steps and a balustrade; *santharitvā majjhe chiddaṃ katvā vaccaṃ kātuṃ*, Horner: "I allow you, monks, to evacuate having spread (something) and made a hole in the middle"; *vaccapādukā* ("privy shoes", foot-rests); a urine trough; scraping sticks and a receptacle for them; a lid; then *Ajjhokāse vaccaṃ karontā sītenapi uṇhenapi kilamanti. "Anujānāmi bhikkhave vaccakuṭin"ti*, Horner: "Evacuating in the open air, they were bothered by the cold and heat. 'I allow, monks, a hut for the privy.'" The hut gets a door with its fittings, plaster, a support for an old monk who "having evacuated, fell down as he was getting up", an enclosure, a porch, gravel and flagstones, a drain (*udakaniddhamana*), a rinsing-water jar (*ācamanakumbhī*), a scoop and rinsing foot-rests. — [GRETIL Cullavagga (Pali)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/2_pali/1_tipit/1_vin/cullav_u.htm); [Horner, Book of the Discipline vol. 5, pp. 196–197](https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.718)
- **The Victorian translators declined to translate this chapter** [RD]: Rhys Davids and Oldenberg give only the first rule ("I allow you, O Bhikkhus, to make water at one side (of the Ārāma)") and then: "[The rest of this chapter is scarcely translateable. It records in like manner the various sanitary difficulties which arose from the living together of a number of Bhikkhus. … The result of the whole is, that the building of privies is enjoined, and all the contrivances, such as seats, doors, steps, plastering, &c., already mentioned with respect to the bath-room, above, V, 14, are here repeated verbatim]". — [Rhys Davids and Oldenberg, Vinaya Texts III (SBE 20), Cullavagga V.35](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.189082)
- **Cullavagga VIII.9–10 (Vin II 221–222), privy etiquette (*vaccakuṭivatta*)** [RD, Pali and Horner]: use in order of arrival, not seniority ("I allow, you monks, to relieve yourselves according to the order of arrival"); *Yo vaccakuṭiṃ gacchati tena bahi ṭhitena ukkāsitabbaṃ. Anto nisinnenapi ukkāsitabbaṃ* (Horner: "Whoever goes to a privy, standing outside should cough", and the one inside coughs back); hang up the robe; enter "carefully and unhurriedly"; do not lift the robe before standing on the foot-rests; do not groan, chew a tooth-stick, miss the trough, spit into the urine trough, use rough scraping wood or drop it into the cesspit; rinse quietly and leave no water in the scoop; wash and sweep a soiled privy, empty the full scraper box, refill the water jar. The Pali of these rules contains no word for any direction. — [GRETIL Cullavagga (Pali)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/2_pali/1_tipit/1_vin/cullav_u.htm); [Horner, Book of the Discipline vol. 5, pp. 309–311](https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.718)
- **Cullavagga V.14 (Vin II 119–121), the hot bath-house (*jantāghara*)** [RD, Pali; Horner and Rhys Davids and Oldenberg]: at the physician Jīvaka's request, because monks at Vesālī had grown heavy and sickly from rich food, *"anujānāmi bhikkhave caṅkamañca jantāgharañcā"ti* (Horner: "a place for pacing up and down in and a bathroom"). It is to be on a raised plinth faced with brick, stone or wood because it "was low to the ground; it was flooded by water", with steps, balustrade, door, a facing for the wall base, a chimney or steam pipe (*dhūmanetta*); *khuddake jantāghare ekamantaṃ aggiṭṭhānaṃ kātuṃ, mahallake majjhe* (Horner: "to make a fireplace at one side of a small bathroom, in the middle of a large one"); clay for the face, a water container and scoop, a floor of brick, stone or wood, a drain (*udakaniddhamana*), bath-stools, an enclosure and a porch. Cullavagga VIII.8 adds bath-house etiquette. No direction word occurs. — [GRETIL Cullavagga (Pali)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/2_pali/1_tipit/1_vin/cullav_u.htm); [Horner, Book of the Discipline vol. 5, Cullavagga V.14](https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.718); [Rhys Davids and Oldenberg, Vinaya Texts III, Cullavagga V.14](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.189082)
- **An archaeologist's summary of the Vinaya building list** [RD]: Marshall: "the buildings which constituted a saṅghārāma were, according to the Vinaya texts, the following: living- and sleeping-quarters for the bhikshus; a refectory or service-hall (upaṭṭhāna-sālā); a fire-hall or kitchen (aggi-sālā); an open pillared pavilion (maṇḍapa); a promenade and cloister for walking exercise (caṅkamana-sālā); a bathroom for hot baths (jantāghara); a kaṭhina-hall for tailoring; a privy; a well and well-house (udapāna-sālā); a store-room (koṭṭhaka); and a provision and drug store (kappiya-kuṭi). … these various parts of the saṅghārāma were not incorporated in a single building but were, for the most part at any rate, detached structures." — [Marshall, Taxila vol. I](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.507389)

### Inferences

- The Vinaya is the earliest Indian text read here that prescribes a purpose-built latrine with a squatting slab, foot-rests, pit, lid, door and washing water, and it does so for a celibate community living permanently in one compound, where going "an arrow's flight" away was impractical and the open air was a hardship ("bothered by the cold and heat").
- Its concerns are smell, flooding, privacy, safety of the old and cleanliness. Where it could have fixed a quarter of the compound it says only "at one side". Had a direction mattered to the redactors, the casuistic style of the chapter would be expected to record it.
- The Vinaya's detached privy and bath-house match what Marshall found in the Taxila monasteries (section 7).

### Gaps

- The Sekhiya training rules on urinating and defecating (standing, on crops, in water) and the Mahāvagga were not read.
- The Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya, the Chinese Vinayas and Yijing's seventh-century account of monastic toilet practice were not consulted; a directional rule in those traditions cannot be excluded.
- Buddhaghosa's commentary (Samantapāsādikā) on Cullavagga V.35 and VIII.10 was not read.

## 7. History: indoor bathing places, drains and latrines from the Harappan cities to the medieval period, and when attached bathrooms and indoor flush toilets became normal in Indian houses

### Takeaway

Built bathing floors with drains inside houses are among the oldest and best-attested features of South Asian urban archaeology (Mohenjo-daro, Harappa and Lothal, with forerunners at Kalibangan; Kenoyer's time frame for the urban phase at Harappa is "between 2600 B.C. and 1900 B.C.", p. 81), in-house latrines are attested there too though their frequency is disputed, and early historic Taxila has soak-wells serving a "privy, bathroom, wash-house or kitchen" in every house; after that the evidence found is mostly monastic and courtly. For ordinary Indian houses, however, a latrine in the house served by water and a sewer or septic tank is recent: service privies and night-soil removal dominated nineteenth-century cities, the toilet entered the Bombay flat in the 1920s–1930s, and the Census counted a latrine of any kind on the premises of only about 24% of households in 1991, 36% in 2001 and 47% in 2011.

### Cited Findings

#### 7a. Harappan (Indus) sites

- **Kenoyer on house bathing areas and wells** [RD]: "Most houses or groups of houses had private bathing areas and latrines as well as private wells." "Bathing platforms with drains were often situated in rooms adjacent to the well (fig. 3.16). The floor of the bath was usually made of tightly fitted bricks, often set on edge, making a watertight floor. A small drain cut through the house wall out into the street directed the dirty water to a larger sewage drain. Much care was taken to ensure that water from the bathing area and latrines did not flow into the rest of the house. Drains and water chutes from the second story were often built inside the wall with an exit opening just above the street drain." — [Kenoyer, Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization, pp. 57–59](https://archive.org/details/ancient-cities-of-the-indus-valley-civilization)
- **Kenoyer on latrines** [RD]: "Many urban dwellers may have walked outside the city wall to the nearby fields to relieve themselves, as is commonly done today in much of Asia. But many houses had latrines that were distinct and separate from the bathing areas. The early excavators at both Mohenjo-daro and Harappa did not pay much attention to this essential feature of the Indus cities, but current excavations at Harappa are finding what appear to be latrines in almost every house. The commodes were made of large jars or sump pots sunk into the floor, and many of them contained a small jar (fig. 3.17) similar to the modern water jar or lota used throughout Pakistan and India for washing after using the toilet. Sometimes these sump pots were connected to a drain to let sewage flow out, and most had a tiny hole at the bottom to let water seep into the ground. … These sump pot latrines were probably cleaned out quite regularly by a special class of laborers". — [Kenoyer, Ancient Cities, p. 60](https://archive.org/details/ancient-cities-of-the-indus-valley-civilization)

### Inferences

- There are two different histories. Bathing floors, drains and (to a debated extent) latrines inside houses existed in the Indus cities and at Bhir Mound, and monasteries and palaces had them throughout; but the Sanskrit conduct literature, composed in between, describes open-air relief as the norm for the householder, and the mass of Indian households had no domestic latrine until the last decades of the twentieth century. Archaeological presence and normative prescription should not be merged.
- The archaeological positions do not show a compass rule: the Taxila monastery bathrooms sit in the south-east, north-east or south-west corner of the court; the Mohenjo-daro bathing floors are "usually set against an outside wall" (a drainage logic); the House XLIX privies stand against a south wall; the Kolhua toilet chamber is on the south wall; the Great Bath drains to the south-west. No pattern can be claimed from so few cases, and none of the archaeologists read proposes one.
- A fixed seat inside a room that a person must face in one direction only became a mass problem when the toilet moved indoors (Bombay flats from the 1920s, middle-class houses and apartments in the later twentieth century). That is the period in which a "Vastu rule for the toilet seat" becomes a practical question, and the scholarship on that transition (Rao, Srinivas) describes the move indoors as a break with older purity practice in which excrement was kept away from the dwelling.
- The caste organisation of excreta removal is continuous in the sources: Kenoyer's "special class of laborers" (an inference of his), Marshall's "town sweepers", the Śūdrakamalākara's castes who sweep privies, the Bombay halalkhore with a separate passage.

### Gaps

- Possehl's *The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective* is access-restricted and was not read; no statement is attributed to him. Jansen was read only through an abstract and search summaries.
- The excavation reports for Lothal (S.R. Rao), Dholavira (R.S. Bisht) and Harappa (HARP) were not opened; Lothal and Dholavira rest on Wikipedia's summaries of them.
- For the long period between the Gupta-period monasteries and the Mughals, no reliable archaeological or textual evidence for latrines in ordinary houses or in Hindu palaces (for example Vijayanagara or Rajput forts) was found; the one search result was an unsourced forum answer and is not used.
- The colonial bungalow evidence is weak (blogs and memoirs). King's *The Bungalow*, Prashad's and Kidambi's work on municipal sanitation and the primary municipal reports were not accessible.
- No source was found that dates when attached bathrooms became standard in public-sector housing (for example Delhi Development Authority flats) or in the National Building Code; "late twentieth century" rests on the Census trend and on Srinivas and Rao as summarised.
- The Census 1981 figure and the split between water closets and pit latrines in 2001 and 2011 could not be verified (search summaries gave inconsistent numbers).

## 8. Conclusion to draw explicitly: were the classical facing rules written for open-air defecation away from the house, and does any classical source apply them to a fixed seat inside a room?

### Takeaway

Yes on the first question and no on the second: every classical facing rule read is embedded in instructions for relieving oneself outdoors and away from the dwelling (cover the ground with grass, avoid paths, fields, ant-hills and river banks, go south-west beyond an arrow's flight), and not one of the sources that do describe a built latrine (Arthaśāstra, Caraka, the Vinaya) attaches a facing direction or a compass position to it. The modern rule that a toilet seat must lie north-south and never face east or west is a twentieth-century transfer of a daytime/night-time posture rule for the open air to a plumbing fixture.

### Cited Findings

- **The facing rule and the outdoor setting occur in the same sentences** [RD]: Baudhāyana 1.5.10.10 ("Having placed dry grass, wood … or a clod of earth (on the ground), let him void faeces or urine, turning his face during the day towards the north and at night towards the south"); Vasiṣṭha 12.13 ("covering the ground with grass … turning towards the north in the day-time"); Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.11.9–15 (go south-west beyond an arrow's flight, then face north by day, "covering … the ground with grass"); Manu 4.45–50 (not on a road, ashes, a cow-pen, ploughed land, an ant-hill, a river bank; cover the ground with sticks, clods, leaves, grass; north by day). — [Bühler, SBE 14 (Baudhāyana, Vasiṣṭha)](https://archive.org/details/sacredlawsofarya00buhliala); [Wilson, Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.11](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/vishnu-purana-wilson/d/doc115988.html); [Bühler, SBE 25, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft)
- **The same texts send the person away from the house** [RD]: Āpastamba 1.31.2, Gautama 9.39, Manu 4.151, Yājñavalkya 1.154, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.11.9 (all quoted in section 2). — [Bühler, SBE 2](https://archive.org/details/sacredlawsofarya00buhliala); [GRETIL Viṣṇupurāṇa, critical ed.](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_viSNupurANa-crit.htm)
- **The rule is suspended in shade and darkness, and Medhātithi defines shade by walls and doors** [RD]: Manu 4.51; Vasiṣṭha 6.13; Medhātithi: "'Shade'—i.e., where the sun's rays are shut out by walls or doors, etc." — [Jha/Medhātithi, verse numbered 4.52](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200156.html)

### Inferences

- No classical source read applies the north-by-day, south-by-night rule, or any ban on facing east or west, to a seat, pan, pit or room. The claim that "Vastu" or "the śāstras" require a north-south toilet seat cannot be supported from the Dharmaśāstra, Purāṇa, Arthaśāstra, Āyurveda, Kāmasūtra or Vinaya passages examined here. (Whether any vāstu manual states such a rule is outside this researcher's scope.)
- The genuine textual root is identifiable and narrow: Manu 4.50 and its parallels (north by day, south at night) plus Manu 4.48 and 4.52 and their parallels (not facing sun, moon, fire, wind, water, cows, Brahmins). The modern formula keeps two compass words from the first and the sun-and-moon motive from the second and discards the rest: the day/night switch, the twilight clause, the exemption for shade, darkness, danger and illness, the alternative authorities (Āpastamba; Yama's west-in-the-forenoon, east-in-the-afternoon), and the fact that the rule addresses a man's posture, not a building.
- If the classical rule is nevertheless applied literally to a fixed indoor seat, three results follow from the texts themselves. (i) No fixed seat can comply, because the prescribed direction reverses at night. (ii) On Medhātithi's gloss and the Kalpataru and Vīramitrodaya reading, a walled and roofed room is "shade" and is exempt, so any direction is permitted. (iii) On Yama's rule an east- or west-facing seat is correct for half of each day. A report can therefore say that the classical sources neither require a north-south seat nor forbid an east- or west-facing one indoors.
- The object-based prohibitions (not facing the sun, moon, fire, a temple or image, cows, Brahmins, water) are the part of the tradition that could be transposed to a house without distortion, for example by not placing a seat so that the user faces the household shrine or the kitchen fire. This is my suggestion for how a traditional reader might reason; no text read makes that transposition.
- Historically, the question could not have mattered to most households before the twentieth century, since a latrine inside an ordinary house was rare until then (section 7).

### Gaps

- The conclusion is limited to the texts opened in this session, in the translations of Bühler, Jolly, Jha, Wilson, Pargiter, Shamasastry, Kangle, Horner and the Gulabkunverba and Bhishagratna Āyurveda translations, and in the GRETIL Sanskrit and Pali e-texts. The medieval digests (Aparārka, Smṛticandrikā, Kṛtyakalpataru, Vīramitrodaya, Parāśaramādhavīya) were seen only through Jha's and Kane's reports; a late digest or regional manual that applies the facing rule to a built privy cannot be excluded.
- Olivelle's translations and annotations were not available to check any of the above.
- Who first formulated the fixed north-south seat rule in print, and when, was not traced.
- The obscure second room-list in Nārada Purāṇa 1.56.587–588, which contains the word *mūtra*, needs a specialist reading or a published translation; it may assign a place to a urinal, and it should be compared with the sixteen-room lists of the astrology and vāstu manuals.
