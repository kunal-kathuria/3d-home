# Classical Vastu stops at the bathroom door

**All five candidate layouts are equally acceptable on the classical evidence, so Sketch B, the designer's practical preference, can be built as drawn.** No vāstu or śilpa treatise, Purāṇic vāstu chapter, astrological compilation, Dharmaśāstra or Arthaśāstra passage found in this research says where a shower, WC, basin, mirror, geyser or floor drain goes inside a bathing room; the texts stop one level up, at which part of a house compound a bath-house or a latrine occupies. At that level they disagree about the bath (east, beside the north-east corner, south-west, west, or outside the house) and agree about the latrine: every text that clearly locates one puts it in the south-western sector, at the south-west corner or between south and south-west (Mayamata 26.214; Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā, vāstu chapter v. 167; Viśvakarmaprakāśa 2.96; Muhūrta Cintāmaṇi 12.21; Samarāṅgaṇa Sūtradhāra 15.27; Rājavallabha 5.43), and one manual of 1571 says "south" for the urinal. That is the part of the plot this bathroom occupies on the drawing, so the modern ban on a toilet in the south-west reverses the texts instead of expressing them. Only two things in the six modern rules tested have a real textual root: the WC user facing north echoes a rule of conduct for relieving oneself in the open (Manu 4.50: north by day, south at night; lifted "in the shade or in darkness" by Manu 4.51), which all five layouts already follow, and keeping a toilet off the centre of the house echoes the rule that guards the central Brahmā block from impurity (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.64). The rest (bathing area in the north-east, north or east of the room; never facing east or west; basin and mirror on the north or east wall; geyser in the south-east; floor falling to the north-east) are extensions of rules about wells, kitchen fires and the natural slope of a building site, or, for the mirror, have no traceable source at all; in the modern books that could be read they surface only from the 1990s, most of them around 2000, and those books contradict one another on every one. Two cautions govern everything below: the room's position and compass orientation come from the drawing as modelled and from the owner's statement that the gate faces north, not from a site survey or a compass reading; and the "zone" the room falls in depends on the method used (a stretched grid, the perimeter list of sixteen rooms, or the modern 22.5-degree wheel) and changes if the room stands about 2 ft from where the drawing puts it.

## The texts cannot tell the five layouts apart, so Sketch B stands

The five layouts differ in three things only: which corner holds the shower booth (south-west in Sketch A and Option 2, south-east in Sketch B and Option 1, north-west in Option 5 revised), which wall carries the basin and mirror (north in the two sketches, west in the three options), and which end of the south wall takes the WC. **No classical source read in this research addresses any of the three.** The house and palace chapters of the Mayamata, Mānasāra, Manuṣyālaya Candrikā, Samarāṅgaṇa Sūtradhāra, Aparājitapṛcchā, Rājavallabha and Vāstumaṇḍana, the house chapter of the Bṛhat Saṃhitā, the vāstu chapters of the Matsya, Agni and Garuḍa Purāṇas and the astrological compilations give positions for a bath-house or a latrine within a compound and nothing more: no fitting, seat, tap, drain or mirror inside the room, and no direction to face while bathing [RD, by keyword search of e-texts and scan OCR, so unusual wording could have been missed] ([Mayamata chs 26–30, Sanskrit](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6); [Bṛhat Saṃhitā, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.txt); [Samarāṅgaṇa Sūtradhāra, chapter index](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%99%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%97%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0)). The only bathing interior any of them describes is the king's shower-house of Samarāṅgaṇa 31.117–142, with piped water, jewelled pillars and music; it orients neither seat, drain nor bather, and the text says it is not to be made for ordinary people [RD, working translation] ([Samarāṅgaṇa ch. 31](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%99%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%97%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0_%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A7)).

What the five layouts share is the one fixture choice with a textual echo: the WC on the south wall, user facing north. About a dozen Dharmasūtras, Smṛtis and Purāṇas prescribe facing north by day when relieving oneself, Manu 4.50 first among them: "Let him void faeces and urine, in the daytime turning to the north, at night turning towards the south" [RD, Bühler] ([Manusmṛti, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, Laws of Manu, p. 136](https://archive.org/details/lawsofmanu00manuuoft)). The rule addresses a person on open ground, it reverses at night, and the next verse suspends it "in the shade or in darkness", which the commentator Medhātithi defines as "where the sun's rays are shut out by walls or doors" [RD, Jha's translation] ([Medhātithi on the exemption verse](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200156.html)). A north-facing seat is therefore a courtesy to the oldest relevant rule, every layout already pays it, and it cannot rank them.

With the texts silent, the decision falls to plumbing and comfort. Sketches A and B and Options 1 and 2 all keep the shower and the WC on the duct wall; Option 5 revised alone takes the shower off it, so its drain has to cross the room. What a modern checklist offers in return is compliance with a rule against taps in the two southern corners of a bathroom, stated in an undated manual, *Advance Vaastu* ("It should never be in the south-east or the south-west direction", pp. 163–164, rule 8) [RD], with no text behind it ([Advance Vaastu](https://archive.org/details/SaralVastuShastraEnglish)). None of the five can meet the modern shower rule in full, because the north-east corner of this room is the doorway, and a consultant working from the modern checklist will rank the layouts differently depending on which manual is open. The mirror entries rest on the researchers' observation that a north-wall mirror faces and reflects the south; applying the same reasoning to a west-wall mirror, which faces east, is this report's step. The sources are Pegrum, Arya, Bansal and the ninth edition of B. N. Reddy, all [RD-snippet], and *Advance Vaastu* and K. S. Ananda Kumar's 1995 paper, both [RD] ([Pegrum 2000](https://archive.org/details/vastuvidyaindian0000pegr); [Arya 2000](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianarto0000arya); [Bansal 2002](https://archive.org/details/vastu0000bans); [B. N. Reddy, 9th ed. 2000](https://archive.org/details/AGlimpseOfPracticalVaastu); [Vasudev (ed.) 1998, pp. 172–174](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev)). On where the WC sits along the south wall the manuals pull both ways as well: Reddy's diagram puts the WC in the south-east of the room, *Advance Vaastu* "in the west, the south or the north-west side of the west".

At house level the classical evidence is kinder to this room than the modern rule is. On the drawing the bathroom lies in the south-western part of the plot, its centre about 12.75 ft west and 6.25 ft south of the plot's centre, well short of the south-west corner itself. The south-west is the one quarter the texts give to a latrine wherever they name one, and Maṇḍana's Vāstumaṇḍana (5.3–5), the one west Indian architectural treatise read that lists the rooms of an ordinary house, puts the bathing rooms in the south-west too, with the toilet place there "outside" [RD, one edition, working translation] ([Vāstumaṇḍana, ed. Bhowmik](https://archive.org/details/SlaE_vastu-mandan-of-sutradhar-mandana-by-dr.-anasuya-bhowmick-motilal-banarasidas-publishers-delhi)). The astrological lists and the south Indian treatises put a bath elsewhere (east, or beside the north-east corner), so on their reckoning only the WC half of a combined bathroom is classically placed. No text read knows a combined bath-and-WC room attached to a bedroom; D. N. Shukla's 1960 survey says of the classical house plans that "attached bath rooms, curtains and the like are not included in them" [RD] ([Shukla 1960, p. 352](https://archive.org/details/irhd_vastu-shastra-vol.-1-hindu-science-of-architecture-by-d.-n.-shukla-1960-lucknow-)).

In plain terms, three things are worth keeping because a text stands behind them. The WC user faces north, which all five layouts do. The bathroom sits in the south-western part of the plot, which the drawing already gives. The centre of the house stays free of impurity, and this needs one check on a measured drawing, because on one way of laying the grid about 2 sq ft of this bathroom, clear floor just inside its door, touches the central block. One further textual rule bears on the duct and has nothing to do with the compass: Arthaśāstra 3.8.9 keeps a house's water-course a set distance from the neighbour's wall, and 3.8.22 doubles the fine when the damage to that wall is done by urine and faeces [RD] ([Kangle, Arthaśāstra Part II](https://archive.org/details/dli.csl.8820)). Everything else on the tested list can be set aside without contradicting any text found: the shower in the north-east, north or east of the room, the ban on facing east or west, the basin and mirror on the north or east wall, the geyser in the south-east, the floor falling to the north-east, and the ban on a toilet in the south-west of the house. In this room the north-east corner is the doorway to the bedroom and the duct is behind the south wall, so a floor falling to the north-east would carry water to the threshold and away from the drain; nothing in the texts stands against falling it to the duct.

**The south-west ban is the clearest case of modern convention overriding the texts.** In the volume of the 1995 Bangalore symposium on Vastu the architect-consultant B. N. Reddy writes that toilets go "in the North-West or the [South-]Eastern part of the buildings, and not in the North-East or South-West" (p. 72) [RD], and *Advance Vaastu* says they "should never be in the centre, the north-east, and the south-west corners" (p. 163) [RD] ([Vasudev (ed.) 1998](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev); [Advance Vaastu](https://archive.org/details/SaralVastuShastraEnglish)). Yet Reddy's own book quotes, without naming its source, the verse that puts the "Toilet … in between South and South west" [RD-snippet]; its wording matches Viśvakarmaprakāśa 2.94–96 as read directly for this research, a match that is this report's comparison of the two ([B. N. Reddy](https://archive.org/details/AGlimpseOfPracticalVaastu); [Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash)). Other contributors to the same 1995 volume keep the south-west: K. R. Dasa Setty ("preferably between the South and South-West", p. 176); T. S. Vasan, for whom the south-west is "ruled by Rahu" and is the "place where the toilet has to be built" (p. 117); B. G. Kadirappa, for whom toilets "must be in the South-West … for a East facing house" (pp. 237–238), which is this house's case; and S. K. Ramachandra Rao, the only contributor to give chapter and verse (Śilpaśāstra-sāra-saṃgraha 9.24–28, p. 24) [RD]. The research traces the ban to the Andhra consultants' doctrine that the south-west must carry weight and hold no pit: Gouru Tirupati Reddy's manual is reported to say that a septic tank must not be in the south-west "as any depression there causes serious consequences" and that "a toilet can be provided in southwest without a septic tank attached to it", but that book could not be opened [REP, unverified web summary] ([Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/419490853/Secret-World-of-Vastu-Thirupati-Reddy-pdf)). The north-west preference that replaced the south-west has no classical source in anything read; the texts put grain, cattle and wealth there. One 1995 contributor attaches "North-West for toilet" to a paragraph that begins "The Matsyapurāṇa further says" (M. V. Janardana, p. 186) [RD], but the Matsya's vāstu chapters as read contain no latrine at all. One likely source of the confusion is a modern Hindi gloss in the Vāsturatnākara that reads Muhūrta Mārtaṇḍa 6.18 as putting the place for urine and faeces in the north-west, against the 1571 author's own commentary, which says south [RD; the link to later practice is an inference] ([Vāsturatnākara](https://archive.org/details/GOhs_vastu-ratnakara-with-ahibala-chakra-shri-vindhyeshvari-prasad-dwivedi); [Muhūrta Mārtaṇḍa with the author's commentary](https://archive.org/details/muhurta-martand-kedar-datt-joshi_202210)).

**The north-facing seat has a genuine root that the modern rule distorts.** The rule is absent from the 1995 symposium volume and from the B. N. Reddy snippets and present in most books read from 2000 on, first in Juliet Pegrum's *Vastu Vidya* ("the north-south line; when sitting you should face north") [RD-snippet] ([Pegrum 2000](https://archive.org/details/vastuvidyaindian0000pegr)). The research infers that its first printing lies in the unread manuals of 1995–1999, an inference from absence in a limited sample. No modern author read cites a text for it, so its link to Manu 4.50 is a strong resemblance and not a documented derivation. The modern books do not even agree that east and west are barred: *Advance Vaastu* asks for the "N/S axis" in rule 2 and lets users face "the north or the west" in rule 6 of the same page [RD], a 2003 book wants the user to "face north, east or northeast", and a 2012 book says "either towards the North or the East" [RD-snippet] ([Advance Vaastu](https://archive.org/details/SaralVastuShastraEnglish); [Ashwini Kumar 2003](https://archive.org/details/unfoldingveilofm0000ashw); [Vastu for Home and Office 2012](https://archive.org/details/vastuforhomeoffi0000unse)). The classical rule itself is set out in the next section. What matters here is that it names north and south as the default directions for a person in the open, never bans east or west as compass points, and is switched off in shade.

**The water-fixture and heater rules are analogies.** The earliest fixture layout read is a diagram in the ninth edition (2000) of B. N. Reddy's *A Glimpse of Practical Vaastu*: "Shower - North east. Wash Basin - East … Wash basin - West (alt position)" [RD-snippet; whether the 1992 first edition had it is unknown] ([B. N. Reddy](https://archive.org/details/AGlimpseOfPracticalVaastu)). The logic later books state is that the north-east is the "water" quarter and the south-east the "fire" quarter. The classical seeds are real but concern other objects: an omen about where a well or tank lies (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.117), the Mayamata's well, cistern and bath-house on the north-east squares of a compound, and the kitchen in the south-east. Sashikala Ananth, who cites the Mayamata, rejected the transfer outright in 1998: sewage "should be carefully selected as the point furthest from water source, and can be on any side", and the text "gives a variety of locations for water source, kitchen, bath areas" [RD] ([Ananth 1998, pp. 112–126](https://archive.org/details/XQxf_vastu-sashikala-ananth)). The moderns also contradict one another, since Bansal's 2002 book puts "the bathtub or the shower in the southwest" as the heaviest object in the room [RD-snippet] ([Bansal 2002](https://archive.org/details/vastu0000bans)). The geyser rule extends the hearth to any heater: Reddy's book keeps "boilers, furnaces" in the south-east, and a 2012 book says the "Geyser should also be installed in southeast of bathroom" [RD-snippet] ([B. N. Reddy](https://archive.org/details/AGlimpseOfPracticalVaastu); [Vastu for Home and Office 2012](https://archive.org/details/vastuforhomeoffi0000unse)). Even the seed is not uniform. The Mayamata calls a cooking place in the north-east "good for all" (27.100), the Mānasāra puts kitchens in the north, north-east or Parjanya square (36.26), and the Kāmikāgama assigns the kitchen by class, north-east for Brahmins and south-east only for Kṣatriyas (35.177–178) [RD] ([Mayamata chs 26–30](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6); [Mānasāra ch. 36, tr. Acharya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manasara-english-translation/d/doc421084.html); [Kāmika Āgama, tr. Sabharathnam](https://archive.org/details/kamika-agama-purva-pada-part-1)).

**The floor-slope rule moves a site rule indoors.** The classical verses judge the natural fall of a plot before building. Varāhamihira grades it by class: "If the ground slopes down towards the north, it is beneficial for Brāhmaṇas; towards the east, for Kṣatriyas; towards the south, for Vaiśyas; and towards the west, for Śūdras" (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.89, Kern 53.91, Bhat's translation; Iyer's translation of the same verse gives the opposite sense and conflicts with the Sanskrit) [RD] ([Bhat, Bṛhat Saṃhitā Part 1](https://archive.org/details/aqbw_brihat-samhita-part-1-by-varahamihira-translated-by-m-ramakrishna-bhat-sans); [Iyer's translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita/d/doc229297.html)). The Mayamata and Mānasāra want a site inclined to the north or east (Mayamata 2.10–15; Mānasāra 3.18–29) [RD], and the Samarāṅgaṇa, as Shukla reports it, one that falls to the north, north-east or east (8.66) [REP: Shukla] ([Dagens 1970](https://archive.org/details/mayamatabronodagens_202002); [Acharya 1934](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.108320); [Shukla 1960, p. 265](https://archive.org/details/irhd_vastu-shastra-vol.-1-hindu-science-of-architecture-by-d.-n.-shukla-1960-lucknow-)). A single eight-direction verdict with the north-east best ("store of jewels") appears, among the texts read, only in the undated Viśvakarmaprakāśa (1.14–16), whose subject is the plot at site examination [RD] ([Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash)). Where the Mayamata fixes actual water outlets it pairs each main building's outlet with its door, and two of the four are on the south and west sides (27.51, 27.54) [RD, working translation] ([Mayamata chs 26–30](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6)). The move indoors was contested from the start. R. Venkateswara Sarma, a traditional practitioner writing in 1995, says the site principle "is not applicable to the main building on any side. Contrary to this, the Vastu pandits are preaching that the flooring should slope towards North-East in each room of the building" (p. 194) [RD] ([Vasudev (ed.) 1998](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev)).

**The centre rule is textual; the north-east ban is an inference from what the texts put there.** No text read forbids a latrine in the north-east in so many words. The texts put the shrine and the well there, and the ban follows from that. The centre is different: the Brahmā block is expressly protected from "dirty things like remnants of food", from pillars, walls, impure vessels and ash (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.64; Viśvakarmaprakāśa 7.108–110), and a WC or drain on it would offend the rule on any reading [RD]. How it bears on this room is taken up in the zone section.

The route by which the package formed, as the research reconstructs it from the books that could be read, runs as follows: one traditional sixteen-room verse and astrological lordships of the directions (Rahu for the south-west, Ketu for the north-east) down to 1995; a "practical" re-zoning by architect-consultants; fixture-level elaboration in the second half of the 1990s; then wholesale copying, with identical sentences recurring between books of 2000 and 2003 and between a 2004 book and *Advance Vaastu*. The reasons offered are planetary, bodily or, after 1990, dressed as ultraviolet and magnetism; one 1995 contributor concedes that the rules "have been laid down by ancient astrologers. The reasons for many of them have not been understood" (C. H. Gopinath Rao, p. 101) [RD] ([Vasudev (ed.) 1998](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev)). A consultant may also raise the side on which a bathroom is attached; here it is west of the guest bedroom. Arya says "Attach bathrooms and toilets to the W of the room", a 1995 contributor and the 2012 book prefer north or east, and Pegrum and Ashwini Kumar advise against attached bathrooms altogether; none cites a text [RD-snippet; RD for the 1995 paper, whose OCR is garbled at that sentence] ([Arya 2000](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianarto0000arya); [Vasudev (ed.) 1998, p. 106](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev); [Vastu for Home and Office 2012](https://archive.org/details/vastuforhomeoffi0000unse); [Pegrum 2000](https://archive.org/details/vastuvidyaindian0000pegr); [Ashwini Kumar 2003](https://archive.org/details/unfoldingveilofm0000ashw)).

### Five different places for the bath

The oldest dated source in the set, Varāhamihira's Bṛhat Saṃhitā of the sixth century, allots only the four corners of a four-winged courtyard house (shrine north-east, kitchen south-east, household goods south-west, wealth and grain north-west: 52.116, Kern 53.118) and has no bathing room, latrine or mirror rule anywhere in its house chapter [RD: Sanskrit, two translations, and the commentary Utpala completed in 966 CE] ([Bṛhat Saṃhitā, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.txt); [Iyer's translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita/d/doc229297.html); [Utpala's commentary](https://archive.org/details/lPan_brihat-samhita-of-sri-varaha-mihira-part-2-with-bhattotpala-commentary-edited-by)). Utpala himself calls the allocation general and quotes a fuller eight-room list from the Kiraṇa-tantra, the same couplet as Agni Purāṇa 106.18–19, which puts a treasure room (*śrī-gṛha*) in the east and still names neither bath nor latrine [RD] ([Agni Purāṇa, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_agnipurANa.txt)). **Any statement that Varāhamihira puts the bathroom in the east or the toilet between south and south-west is unsupported by his text.**

"Bath in the east" is the formula of the astrological school. It stands in the vāstu chapter of the Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā (v. 164: *aindryāṃ diśi snāna-geham*) [RD, three editions], in the Kāśyapa Saṃhitā (37.75) [REP: an editor's cross-reference, and a quotation in a 1603 commentary that was itself read directly], in Nārada Saṃhitā 31.50 and Nārada Purāṇa 1.56.585 [RD], in Viśvakarmaprakāśa 2.94 [RD, three printings], and in two dated manuals for choosing auspicious times, the Muhūrta Mārtaṇḍa of 1571 (6.18) and the Muhūrta Cintāmaṇi of 1600 (12.21) [RD] ([Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā, ed. Shastri](https://archive.org/details/rbkt-vashishtha-samhita-by-vriddha-vashishtha-edited-wi); [Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash); [Muhūrta Cintāmaṇi with the Pīyūṣadhārā](https://archive.org/details/muhurta-chintamani-kedar-datt-joshi); [Muhūrta Mārtaṇḍa](https://archive.org/details/muhurta-martand-kedar-datt-joshi_202210)). The list grew in layers: four corners in the sixth century, eight directions without bath or latrine by 966, eight directions with a bath in the east securely by 1571, and the full sixteen with a latrine between south and south-west securely by 1600–1603. How much older the undated saṃhitās are could not be established. The printed Viśvakarmaprakāśa, to which popular books credit the "sixteen rooms", names only eleven, and its 2017 editor says verses are lost and fills the gap from another text; the "sixteen" of Nārada Purāṇa 1.56.585 counts house types, not rooms [RD]. The commentators treat all of these as utility buildings (*upakaraṇa-gṛha*) placed round a main house "once the well has been made", and the 1571 author allows them to be fitted "inside the house or outside it" when space is short [RD, working translations]. Even inside this school the east is contested: a "Viśvakarmā" half-verse quoted in 1603 puts the water place in the north and a treasure room in the east, and it is not in the printed Viśvakarmaprakāśa [RD].

Every other family puts the bath somewhere else. The Matsya Purāṇa (256.35) says the bathing pavilion is to be built outside (*bahiḥ*), with no direction, and a 1917 translation and a 1935 study read it the same way [RD] ([Matsya Purāṇa, Ānandāśrama ed.](https://archive.org/details/ozcr-matsya-purana-aa-54-ananda-ashram); [1917 translation](https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.118363); [Dikshitar 1935, p. 108](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.68949)). The south Indian treatises put it beside the north-east corner: the Mayamata at Uditi, the north-side square next to that corner (26.216), and the Mānasāra at Aditi (36.41; palace bath-house at Aditi or Udita, 40.103) [RD] ([Mayamata chs 26–30](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6); [Mānasāra ch. 36](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manasara-english-translation/d/doc421084.html); [Mānasāra ch. 40](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manasara-english-translation/d/doc421088.html)). The Kerala texts give a bath only to kings, at Parjanya on the east side by the same corner (Manuṣyālaya Candrikā 7.34; Śilparatna I.5.88–90; Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati, Kriyāpāda 25.81), and for an ordinary house provide a tank and the rule that bathing and drinking water be kept separate (7.29) [RD] ([Manuṣyālaya Candrikā](https://archive.org/details/sthapatyavedmanushyalayacandrika); [Śilparatna](https://archive.org/details/TSS075TheSilparatnaOfSriKumaraKSambasivaSastriVolume11922_201809); [Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.345368)). The Kāmikāgama has a bath hall in the west, at Varuṇa (35.187), a reading shared by one Mayamata manuscript [RD, Sabharathnam's translation] ([Kāmika Āgama](https://archive.org/details/kamika-agama-purva-pada-part-1)). The west Indian architectural treatises go to the south-west: Bhoja's eleventh-century Samarāṅgaṇa Sūtradhāra puts the palace baths and shower-houses in a garden behind the south-west end of the west side (15.26–31), and Maṇḍana's Vāstumaṇḍana puts bathing in the south-west of an ordinary house (5.4) and on the south-west corner square of a palace (5.21) [RD] ([Samarāṅgaṇa ch. 15](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%99%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%97%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0_%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A7%E0%A5%AB); [Vāstumaṇḍana](https://archive.org/details/SlaE_vastu-mandan-of-sutradhar-mandana-by-dr.-anasuya-bhowmick-motilal-banarasidas-publishers-delhi)). The dharma texts stand apart from all of them. The daily bath is taken at a river, tank or spring (Manu 4.203), a bath at home with drawn water is a fallback (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.11.25–26), and nothing in the Dharmaśāstra or Āyurveda passages read ties a bathing place to the east or north-east [RD] ([Manusmṛti, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Kane, History of Dharmaśāstra II.1, p. 660](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.441002)).

### South-west for the latrine, where one is named at all

Where a latrine is located, the sector is constant. The Mayamata's general rule for a house compound puts "the lying-in room and that of excrement" at Nirṛti, the south-west (26.214: *nirṛtau sūtigṛhaṃ ca varcasaḥ*), and declares the scheme common to all four classes (26.219) [RD, working translation; a Hindi rendering construes the verse the same way] ([Mayamata, Dagens ed. vol. 2](https://archive.org/details/Xveg_mayamata-by-bruno-dagens-vol.-2-motilal-banarasi-das); [Mayamata chs 26–30](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6)). The astrological school puts the "house for voiding faeces" (*purīṣa-tyāga-mandira*) between south and south-west: Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā v. 167, Viśvakarmaprakāśa 2.96 and Muhūrta Cintāmaṇi 12.21, with Hindi glosses in several printings to the same effect [RD] ([Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā, ed. Shastri](https://archive.org/details/rbkt-vashishtha-samhita-by-vriddha-vashishtha-edited-wi); [Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash); [Muhūrta Cintāmaṇi](https://archive.org/details/muhurta-chintamani-kedar-datt-joshi)). The 1571 Muhūrta Mārtaṇḍa says "in the south" for the urinal (6.18, with the author's own commentary) [RD]. The palace chapters agree. Samarāṅgaṇa 15.27 has the refuse or privy place (*avaskara*) on Pitṛ's square, the south-west corner [RD, one e-text; S. K. Sharma's translation, read in a scan without legible verse numbers, attaches a "privy, closet or a place for faeces" to the Mṛga and Pitṛ squares]. Rājavallabha 5.43 has the "faeces-house" (*varco-gṛha*) "in the corner of the night-walker", the south-west [RD]. Vāstumaṇḍana 5.21 has the toilet place on the square beside that corner, "outside" [RD] ([Samarāṅgaṇa ch. 15](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%99%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%97%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0_%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A7%E0%A5%AB); [Sharma's translation](https://archive.org/details/samarangana-sutradhara-an-ancient-treatise-on-arch); [Rājavallabha, 1948 ed., p. 79](https://archive.org/details/fayt_prasada-mandanam-and-rajavallabha-mandanam-by-sutradhara-mandan-sanskrit-calcutt); [Vāstumaṇḍana](https://archive.org/details/SlaE_vastu-mandan-of-sutradhar-mandana-by-dr.-anasuya-bhowmick-motilal-banarasidas-publishers-delhi)).

Three qualifications keep this from being a simple rule. First, most texts name no latrine at all: the Bṛhat Saṃhitā, the Matsya, Agni and Garuḍa vāstu chapters, Mānasāra chapters 36 and 40, the Manuṣyālaya Candrikā and the other Kerala texts, and the ordinary-house chapters of the Samarāṅgaṇa and Aparājitapṛcchā [RD, keyword searches]. Second, two passages are insecure. Mayamata 29.117, in a palace enclosure, has a *vacor-geha* beside the well and a water machine at the north-east corner; Dagens's apparatus marks the word as a correction, the 1919 edition prints it with a question mark, and only a Hindi rendering takes it as *varco-gṛha*, a latrine. If accepted it would put a latrine in the north-east against 26.214, and the research builds nothing on it [RD] ([Mayamata, Dagens ed. vol. 2](https://archive.org/details/Xveg_mayamata-by-bruno-dagens-vol.-2-motilal-banarasi-das); [1919 Trivandrum ed.](https://archive.org/details/TSS065MayamatamTGSastri1919_201809)). The Kāmikāgama's *maitra-sthāna* in the south-west (35.180) is a "reception hall" in Sabharathnam's translation but reads as the place of evacuation if *maitra* has the sense it has in Manu 4.152, and the same Āgama (3.17) allows "the call of nature and bath" at home in a *maitra-pada*, which its translator takes as Mitra's square. No commentary was available to settle either [RD; the evacuation reading is the researcher's] ([Kāmika Āgama](https://archive.org/details/kamika-agama-purva-pada-part-1)). Third, the latrine of these texts is a detached building of a large compound or palace. The Vāstumaṇḍana says "outside" twice, the Mayamata speaks of "the first enclosure or the second", and no vāstu text read gives a distance; the only measured distances are for the scavengers' hamlet and the cremation ground (Mayamata 9.96–98) [RD] ([Mayamata, Dagens ed. vol. 1](https://archive.org/details/GwHQ_mayamata-by-bruno-dagens-vol.-1-motilal-banarasi-das)).

The dharma texts explain the quarter. They send the householder out: "far from his house, having gone towards the south or south-west" (Āpastamba 1.11.31.2, Bühler), "far from his dwelling" (Manu 4.151), "to the south-west … beyond an arrow's flight" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.11.9, working translation of the critical text; Wilson's printed translation has "south-east" and should not be quoted for this) [RD] ([Bühler, Sacred Laws of the Āryas](https://archive.org/details/sacredlawsofarya00buhliala); [Viṣṇu Purāṇa, critical ed.](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_viSNupurANa-crit.htm)). The built latrine of the vāstu texts looks like that outdoor practice brought inside the compound wall at the same bearing; no text read states the link, and it is the researchers' inference. Living tradition matches. Descriptions of Kerala compounds from 1906 to 1982 show bathing at a tank with a bathing shed, a well at the kitchen, and either no latrine or a detached one "at a distance from the house", recorded in the south-western section beside the cowshed. Tamil Brahmin row houses put the latrines at the far rear of the plot, where scavengers reached them from a back door, and a 1972 gazetteer says of the western part of one district that "in most of the houses there are no latrines" [RD] ([Travancore State Manual 1906](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.29166); [Bernier 1982](https://archive.org/details/dita_temple-arts-of-kerela-ronald-m.-bernier); [Bhooshan and Misra 1979](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.111706); [Reiniche 1981](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.490224); [Ramanathapuram gazetteer 1972](https://archive.org/details/dli.ministry.08709)). A latrine inside an ordinary house is recent. The toilet is reported to have moved into the Bombay flat in the 1920s and 1930s [REP], and the Census counted a latrine of any kind on the premises of 36.4% of households in 2001 and 46.9% in 2011 [RD in Wikipedia's table, which cites the Census] and of about 24% in 1991 [REP] ([Art Deco Mumbai essay](https://www.artdecomumbai.com/research/the-modern-bathroom-and-the-home-a-story-of-style-aspiration-and-social-mobility/); [Census table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_states_ranking_by_availability_of_toilets); [1991 figure](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/the-uneven-progress-of-sanitation-in-india)). The modern manual-writers concede the gap themselves: "In the ancient times, toilet was not an integral part of residential vaastu" [RD-snippet] ([Ashwini Kumar 2003](https://archive.org/details/unfoldingveilofm0000ashw)).

### Water in the north-east is a preference about wells, not a law

The seed of the modern "water belongs in the north-east" is one verse of Varāhamihira (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.117, Kern 53.119) listing what follows from water lying in each of the eight directions: north brings wealth, north-east sons, and the other six bring harm. His word is *salila*, water; Utpala and both translators take it as a well or tank on the site, and it was a later author, in 1571, who applied the verse to the household water store [RD] ([Bṛhat Saṃhitā, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.txt); [Iyer's translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita/d/doc229297.html); [Muhūrta Mārtaṇḍa](https://archive.org/details/muhurta-martand-kedar-datt-joshi_202210)). The Mayamata gives the idea its fullest form, with the well at the north-east corner (26.216) and the water store and basin on the two inner north-east squares, Āpavatsa and Āpa (27.108), and the Manuṣyālaya Candrikā ranks a well at Mīna as "the very best" (7.28); that Mīna is the north-east corner sign is the researcher's deduction from a commentary's map of the signs [RD, working translations] ([Mayamata chs 26–30](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6); [Manuṣyālaya Candrikā](https://archive.org/details/sthapatyavedmanushyalayacandrika)).

The same corpus contradicts it at every turn. Water belongs in the west, the quarter of Varuṇa, lord of the waters, in the Garuḍa Purāṇa's hermitage (1.46.16), the Aparājitapṛcchā's monastery and palace (85.16; 82.6), the Samarāṅgaṇa's palace (ch. 15), the Kāmikāgama's house (35.180) and one line of the Viśvakarmaprakāśa ("water on the west is best", 7.115), although Varāhamihira's verse makes water in the west a cause of women's misconduct [RD] ([Garuḍa Purāṇa, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_garuDapurANa.txt); [Aparājitapṛcchā, ed. Jugnu](https://archive.org/details/aparajitaprccha-shrikrishna-jugnu); [Kāmika Āgama](https://archive.org/details/kamika-agama-purva-pada-part-1); [Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash)). Maṇḍana keeps the water house in the south (Vāstumaṇḍana 5.4), and the Matsya Purāṇa keeps water beside the south-east kitchen (256.34) [RD] ([Vāstumaṇḍana](https://archive.org/details/SlaE_vastu-mandan-of-sutradhar-mandana-by-dr.-anasuya-bhowmick-motilal-banarasidas-publishers-delhi); [Matsya Purāṇa ch. 256](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AA%E0%A5%81%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%83_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AB%E0%A5%AC)). The Mānasāra puts the wells of all classes in the south-east sector (36.27), which the Manuṣyālaya Candrikā (7.30) and an appendix to the Mayamata call a cause of fire and fear; Acharya, the Mānasāra's translator, warns that "one can hardly be perfectly sure of the interpretation" of a defective text [RD] ([Mānasāra ch. 36](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manasara-english-translation/d/doc421084.html); [Acharya 1934](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.108320)). At Mānasāra 40.100 Acharya's translation digs the palace tank "in the north-west or the south-west" while the Sanskrit e-text reads north-west or south-east (*anile cāgnike*). The two cannot be reconciled without his printed Sanskrit and apparatus, which could not be read, and neither reading is north-east [RD for both; unresolved] ([Mānasāra ch. 40](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manasara-english-translation/d/doc421088.html); [Mānasāra, Sanskrit e-text](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%A8%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D)). The Mayamata itself says that in a village reservoirs go wherever water is wanted (9.88) and that in a temple enclosure the well, tank and pond can be placed anywhere (23.92) [RD, Dagens's French] ([Dagens 1970](https://archive.org/details/mayamatabronodagens_202002)). The Kerala texts disagree on how to rank well positions, one making the north-east corner sign best and another the sign next to it (Manuṣyālaya Candrikā 7.28 against Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati 23.31–32) [RD] ([Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati, 1989 reprint](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.383645)).

Nor does the deity grid make the north-east a water corner in any simple way. In Varāhamihira's 81-square grid the north-east corner god is Śikhin, Fire (52.43); Īśa holds that corner in the Mayamata, the Mānasāra and the Agni Purāṇa; Āpa and Āpavatsa, the "waters", sit on the inner north-east squares; and the lord of the waters is in the middle of the west in every list read [RD] ([Bṛhat Saṃhitā, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.htm); [Kramrisch, The Hindu Temple, pp. 90–92](https://archive.org/details/hindu-temple-kramrisch)). The one explicit five-element diagram found, the Mayamata's nine-square *pīṭha* (7.24), has water north-east, fire south-east, ether south-west, wind north-west and earth in the centre, and Dagens sets that small diagram apart from the grids that govern houses [RD] ([Dagens 1970](https://archive.org/details/mayamatabronodagens_202002)). Sashikala Ananth's 1998 chart coincides with it (this report's comparison); the chart consultants usually show swaps earth and ether; and the modern books read give six mutually incompatible element charts in all. No scholarly study tracing the usual one was found [RD for the charts] ([Ananth 1998](https://archive.org/details/XQxf_vastu-sashikala-ananth); [Advance Vaastu, p. 12](https://archive.org/details/SaralVastuShastraEnglish); [MahaVastu cards](https://archive.org/details/mahavastu-cards-1)). All of this concerns a well, tank or cistern in a compound. None of it is a statement about taps, showers or basins.

### Mirrors are lucky objects and palace luxuries, never wall rules

In every classical text searched the mirror is an auspicious emblem, a ritual implement, a toilet article or a piece of furniture. The Bṛhat Saṃhitā mentions mirrors only in similes, iconography and festival decoration, never in its house chapter. The Mānasāra gives a mirror's dimensions and handle (50.111–132). The Samarāṅgaṇa defines a "mirror house" whose walls are faced with polished metal as a palace luxury, with no direction (18.51). The Viśvakarmaprakāśa lists a mirror among lucky sights (1.76) [RD] ([Bṛhat Saṃhitā, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.htm); [Acharya 1934](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.108320); [Samarāṅgaṇa ch. 18](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%99%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%97%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0_%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A7%E0%A5%AE); [Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash)). The only mirrors that come with a direction are ritual: a small gold mirror-emblem laid first, at the east, in the foundation deposit of a Śiva temple (Mayamata 12.33–35), and a mirror among the toilet articles offered to Śiva's image, where the words "in the north" attach to the mirror on one reading and only to the pigment in Gangadharan's translation (Agni Purāṇa 78.43–44) [RD] ([Dagens 1970](https://archive.org/details/mayamatabronodagens_202002); [Agni Purāṇa ch. 78](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-agni-purana/d/doc1083308.html)). Searches of the Mayamata's house chapters, the Manuṣyālaya Candrikā, the Vāstumaṇḍana, the Vāsturatnākara and the Bṛhadvāstumālā found no placement rule; a late regional manual containing one cannot be excluded [RD, keyword searches].

The wall rule's modern history is short. It is absent from the 1990s sources read. The 1995 symposium instead shows the mirror entering Vastu as a remedy, with an explicit parallel to what Chinese and Japanese architects do (U. S. Pulippani, p. 169), and with a different directional rule, that a mirror "should not reflect South, South-East or North-West" (K. S. Ananda Kumar, pp. 172–174) [RD] ([Vasudev (ed.) 1998](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev)). "Mirrors should be kept on the north or east walls only" first appears in the sample in 2000, in Pegrum's book, whose American edition is subtitled "The Indian Feng Shui", and in Rohit Arya's; Arya and Richard Craze both say in the same years that Vaastu has no native mirror cure [RD-snippet] ([Pegrum 2000](https://archive.org/details/vastuvidyaindian0000pegr); [Arya 2000](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianarto0000arya); [Craze 2001](https://archive.org/details/vaastuindianspir0000craz)). From 2001 the borrowing is open: A. K. Bansal publishes from "Fengshui Point", and A. R. Hari's 2004 book has a chapter on correcting Vastu defects through Feng Shui with the Pakua mirror [RD-snippet] ([Bansal 2001](https://archive.org/details/isbn_9788190125741); [Hari 2004](https://archive.org/details/vaastucorrection0000hari)). The first author of the "north or east wall" wording was not identified, and no scholarly study of the rule's history was found.

### The facing rule belongs to open ground; the law books regulate nuisance

The majority rule of the conduct literature is uniform: face north by day and at twilight, south at night, for both urine and faeces. It stands in Manu 4.50, Gautama 9.41–43, Vasiṣṭha 6.10 and 12.13, Baudhāyana 1.5.10.10, Viṣṇu Smṛti 60.2, Yājñavalkya 1.16, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.11.14 and five other Purāṇas, and in the Kāmikāgama's conduct chapter (3.8–9) [RD] ([Manusmṛti, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_manusmRti.htm); [Bühler, Sacred Laws of the Āryas](https://archive.org/details/sacredlawsofarya00buhliala); [Jolly, Institutes of Vishnu](https://archive.org/details/institutesofvish00joll)). The same sentences fix the setting: cover the bare ground with grass or leaves, avoid roads, ploughed fields, ant-hills and river banks, wrap the head. A separate rule forbids facing or looking at the sun, moon, fire, wind, water, cows, Brahmins and images of gods (Manu 4.48, 4.52) [RD]. The reasons given are religious: "his life will not be injured" (Vasiṣṭha 6.10), "the intellect … perishes" (Manu 4.52). None of the texts or commentators read offers a physical mechanism such as magnetism.

The texts do not agree with one another, and none clearly bans east and west as such. A verse quoted in Āpastamba (1.11.31.1) assigns directions by act and not by hour: "void faeces facing the south, discharge urine facing the north" [RD, Bühler]. A verse of Yama cited in a medieval commentary prescribes "facing the west in the forenoon, and the east in the afternoon, and the south at night" [REP: Ganganath Jha's notes] ([Jha, comparative notes](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200154.html)). The one possible compass ban is a half-verse of the Vāmana Purāṇa (14.30) that the researcher reads, tentatively and without a published translation, as "nor having turned to the eastern or western quarter" [RD Sanskrit; reading uncertain] ([Vāmana Purāṇa, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_vAmanapurANa1-69.htm)). The exemption is as old as the rule: "In the shade or in darkness a Brahmana may, both by day and at night, do it, assuming any position he pleases" (Manu 4.51, Bühler; identical in Vasiṣṭha 6.13, where Bühler glosses the shade as that "of houses, clouds, and so forth") [RD]. Medieval authorities differed on its width. One line, reported from the Kalpataru and Vīramitrodaya, confines the direction rule to open daylight and moonlit nights; another, reported from Kullūka, the Smṛticandrikā and Mādhava, exempts only someone who cannot tell the directions [REP: Jha] ([Jha on the exemption verse](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc200156.html)). On the first reading a roofed bathroom is exempt, and on the second it is not. Either way three things follow from the texts' own terms: no fixed seat can obey a rule that reverses at night, north is the best-attested single choice, and an east- or west-facing seat indoors is not forbidden by anything read.

Where classical sources do describe a built latrine, the compass disappears. The Arthaśāstra lists "a latrine and a bath-room" among the fittings of royal store buildings (2.5.6, Kangle), treats the refuse pit, waste-water drain and well of an urban house as matters of set-back and nuisance between neighbours (3.8.6–23), and names the latrine among the parts of a dwelling used in common (3.8.28, Kangle; Shamasastry's older translation has no latrine in that verse and reads 3.8.6 as a duty to provide a dung-hill, where Kangle reads a restriction on siting) [RD] ([Kangle, Arthaśāstra Part II](https://archive.org/details/dli.csl.8820); [Shamasastry 3.8](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/kautilya-arthashastra/d/doc366091.html)). Where the same work zones a royal city by direction (2.4.7–15) it places "the places for sacrifices and for water" north-east of the palace and no latrine, drain or bath anywhere. Caraka's house for a patient under treatment is "furnished with a water storage, mortar and pestle, latrine, bath room and kitchen", with no position given (Sūtrasthāna 15.6) [RD, in translation only] ([Caraka Saṃhitā, Sūtrasthāna 15](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/charaka-samhita-english/d/doc627525.html)). The Buddhist monastic code authorises a privy hut with a floor slab, foot-rests, door and washing water, and its only locational rule is "at one side" of the monastery (Cullavagga V.35) [RD, Pali and Horner] ([Horner, Book of the Discipline vol. 5](https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.718)). Archaeology shows the same indifference in the reports read: bathing floors and latrines inside houses are attested in the Indus cities, and the bathrooms of the Taxila monasteries sit in the south-east, north-east or south-west corner of the court, with no directional pattern proposed by the excavators [RD] ([Kenoyer, Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization, pp. 57–60](https://archive.org/details/ancient-cities-of-the-indus-valley-civilization); [Marshall, Taxila vol. I](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.507389)).

## One room gets four zone answers, and two feet changes them

Every figure in this section is arithmetic on the drawing as modelled, not on a survey: a plot 67 ft east–west by 28 ft north–south, taken as square to the compass on the owner's statement that the gate faces north, and a 6 ft square room spanning 17.75 to 23.75 ft from the west boundary with its south wall 4.75 ft inside the south boundary. The calculations are this report's and follow the method used in the research.

Classically a zone is a square of a grid. The site is oriented by the shadow of a gnomon, not by a magnetic needle (Mayamata 6.1–13), and divided by straight lines into 64 or 81 squares whose deities name the zones (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.42–56) [RD] ([Mayamata chs 6–10](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A6%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A7%E0%A5%A6); [Bṛhat Saṃhitā, GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.htm)). The texts expect near-square sites: length exceeds breadth by a tenth to a quarter (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.13), and for a site twice as long as wide the Mayamata grids only a near-square part and leaves the rest out (27.112–113, 27.120–122) [RD, working translation] ([Mayamata chs 26–30](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%A7%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%AF_%E0%A5%A8%E0%A5%AC-%E0%A5%A9%E0%A5%A6)). This plot is 2.39 times as long as wide, beyond anything the texts tabulate, so every method below is an extrapolation. No text read divides a house into equal angles from its centre; the wheel of sixteen 22.5-degree sectors is a modern consultants' device [REP for its MahaVastu description] ([MahaVastu, sixteen zones](http://uk.mahavastu.com/power-of-16-mahavastu-zones-uk.html)).

**The classical readings agree on one thing only: the room is in the south-western part of the site.** It is not on the squares the texts name for a latrine (the Mayamata's Nirṛti corner, the Samarāṅgaṇa's Pitṛ, the Vāstumaṇḍana's Mṛga); on a stretched grid it stands two to three columns in from that corner. The perimeter reading is the friendliest, since it drops the room into the very slot the astrological verse gives to the latrine. The modern wheel's "west-south-west" has no counterpart in any text read. MahaVastu-branded pages are reported to name south-south-west, west-north-west and east-south-east as the toilet zones [REP], and the brand's own card deck gives "IDEAL-ACTIVITY: TOILET" to south-south-west [RD], so a consultant of that school will not find this room in one of its toilet zones, while a consultant using eight directions will call it south-west, which one modern school forbids and another requires ([MahaVastu cards](https://archive.org/details/mahavastu-cards-1); [DNA India report](https://dnaindia.com/lifestyle/report-mahavastu-tips-place-toilet-at-the-right-place-to-boost-your-energy-3041664)). The verdict on the same room depends on the school before it depends on the house.

The central block needs a measured check, not a conclusion. The rule is real: "A house-owner wishing for happiness should guard very carefully Brahman … situated at the centre of the house", for its "affliction by dirty things like remnants of food, would harm the owner" (Bṛhat Saṃhitā 52.64, Kern 53.66, Bhat's translation), and the Viśvakarmaprakāśa adds that no pillar or wall is to stand there and that it is to be kept from impure vessels and ash (7.108–110) [RD] ([Bhat, Bṛhat Saṃhitā Part 1](https://archive.org/details/aqbw_brihat-samhita-part-1-by-varahamihira-translated-by-m-ramakrishna-bhat-sans); [Viśvakarmaprakāśa, 2017 ed.](https://archive.org/details/vishwakarma-prakash)). It attaches to the central block of the whole house site, nine squares of 81 or four of 64, not to the middle of each room. On the stretched 81-square grid, and equally on a simple three-by-three division, a patch of this bathroom about 1.4 ft square, 2.0 sq ft, lies on that block. The patch is the room's north-east corner, which in all five layouts is clear floor just inside the door: no WC, drain, shower or basin stands on it. On the stretched 64-square grid there is no contact at all. A first pass in the research, which placed the room 9 in further east and 3 in further north, put 3.6 sq ft on the block; a shift that small nearly doubling the figure shows how soft it is. Whether walls on the central block matter is a question about the whole house plan, which the brief does not give, and not about this bathroom's fixtures.

Two conventions can also shift the reading. A consultant who lays the grid over the built house instead of the plot, which the tradition allows (the diagram "is coterminous with the building site, or with the extent of the Prāsāda", in Kramrisch's summary), will get different numbers. A grid centred further west or south than the plot's centre puts more of the bathroom on the central block, and a smaller grid shrinks the block; the brief gives no outline of the built house, so neither effect could be sized [RD] ([Kramrisch, The Hindu Temple, pp. 50–51](https://archive.org/details/hindu-temple-kramrisch)). And Maṇḍana defines "east" for his room list as the side the front door is on, not the sunrise (Vāstumaṇḍana 5.1–2) [RD] ([Vāstumaṇḍana](https://archive.org/details/SlaE_vastu-mandan-of-sutradhar-mandana-by-dr.-anasuya-bhowmick-motilal-banarasidas-publishers-delhi)). Because this house fronts east the two coincide, provided the main door is on the east front; a reader who counts from the north gate instead turns his list a quarter-turn and reads the room's quarter differently.

## What was read, what was reported, and what stays unverified

The classical findings rest on passages opened directly in a named edition, e-text or scan, most of them in Sanskrit, with the English supplied either by a named translator (Iyer, Bhat, Acharya, Dagens in French, Bühler, Jolly, Jha, Kangle, Shamasastry, Sabharathnam, Horner) or by the researchers as working translations. The second kind needs a consultant's check, above all for the Mayamata: the Sanskrit and verse numbers of Dagens's edition were legible, but his English could not be read in either scan, so no wording here is his [CNV]. The negative findings (no fixture rule, no mirror rule, no facing rule for bathing, no slope rule for a room) come from keyword searches of e-texts and imperfect scan OCR. They establish that the rules are absent from the chapters named, not from every manuscript or every late regional manual. Page numbers given for scanned books come from the scans' OCR, and those for Kramrisch can be a page out.

Several readings are textually insecure and are flagged where they are used. In the Mayamata, the bath "at Uditi" in 26.216 has a manuscript variant that would read "there", at the north-east corner; "at Uditi" in 29.39 is an editor's correction of manuscripts that say "in the north"; the western bath of 29.50 is likewise a correction; and the possible north-east latrine of 29.117 rests on an emendation [RD] ([Mayamata, Dagens ed. vol. 2](https://archive.org/details/Xveg_mayamata-by-bruno-dagens-vol.-2-motilal-banarasi-das)). In the Mānasāra, the tank of 40.100 is south-west in the translation and south-east in the e-text; "bath-rooms" at 36.81–82 corresponds to "place" (*sthāna*, not *snāna*) in the e-text; and the south-east wells of 36.27 contradict the rest of the southern tradition [RD]. The Vāstumaṇḍana was read in one edition, and its key terms are interpreted: *deha-cintā-pada*, literally "place for care of the body", is taken as the toilet place on the strength of a parallel term glossed as covering both washing and relieving nature, and could mean a general ablution-and-latrine place; and the compound rendered "bathing and childbed" in 5.4 is awkward Sanskrit, so the south-west bath of an ordinary house rests on a best reading. The Samarāṅgaṇa was read in one e-text of unstated edition. Agni Purāṇa 105.28 is corrupt, with three manuscript variants, and the "washing place in the east" is a tentative reading. The Nārada list is corrupt and its "urine" room has no recoverable position. The Kāśyapa Saṃhitā verses and the Kiraṇa-tantra list are known only through quotation. The astrological saṃhitās and the Viśvakarmaprakāśa could not be dated.

Three published translations should not be quoted on the points at issue. Wilson's Viṣṇu Purāṇa has "south-east" and "into the courtyard" at 3.11.8–10 where the Sanskrit has south-west and "not into the courtyard". Iyer's Bṛhat Saṃhitā makes the low side of a plot a cause of suffering at 53.91 where the Sanskrit and Bhat have it desirable. Shamasastry's Arthaśāstra turns 3.8.6 into a duty to provide a dung-hill and has no latrine at 3.8.28, and he calls his own rendering of that chapter "only tentative" [RD for all three comparisons] ([Wilson, Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.11](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/vishnu-purana-wilson/d/doc115988.html); [Iyer's translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita/d/doc229297.html); [Shamasastry 3.8](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/kautilya-arthashastra/d/doc366091.html)). Patrick Olivelle's translations of Manu, the Dharmasūtras and the Arthaśāstra could not be opened, so nothing is attributed to him [CNV]. Kullūka's commentary on Manu, the medieval digests and the Yama and Śaṅkha verses are known only through Jha's and Kane's reports [REP].

The modern genealogy is the weakest link in dating, though not in direction. Lending-library books (B. N. Reddy, Pegrum, Arya, Bansal, Cox, Ashwini Kumar, Hari, the 2012 Vij Books title) were read only as search-inside snippets of about a hundred characters [RD-snippet]. B. N. Reddy's fixture diagram is attested only for the ninth edition of 2000. *Advance Vaastu*, the fullest statement of the modern rule set that could be read in full, prints no author or date. **The manuals most likely to hold the first printing of the WC-orientation and mirror rules were not read at all**: Gouru Tirupati Reddy (Telugu 1987, English 1994), Derebail Muralidhar Rao (1995), B. Niranjan Babu (1997), Bhojraj Dwivedi (1997), and N. H. Sahasrabudhe and R. D. Mahatme (1998), along with the vernacular manuals of the same years. Their existence is established from a library bibliography of Vastu literature; their contents are not [CNV] ([Bibliography of Vastu Shastra Literature, 1834–2009](https://archive.org/details/CollVastuShastraBiblio)). Reading them would move dates earlier. It is unlikely to supply a Sanskrit source, since every book that could be read cites, at most, one unreferenced verse and a list of treatise names.

On the scholarly side the most important unverified item is Vibhuti Chakrabarti's *Indian Architectural Theory: Contemporary Uses of Vastu Vidya* (1998), an academic study of how the texts, modern architects and consultants each use these ideas. The book could not be accessed. Its argument is known here only through Wikipedia's summary, a publisher's description and snippets of the related *Building Jaipur*, which says consultants tend "to reduce vastu vidya to a set of neat programmatic prescriptions"; what Chakrabarti reports about toilet and bathroom placement remains unverified [CNV; REP for the summaries; RD-snippet for *Building Jaipur*] ([Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books?id=Oo-dCM_pp7wC); [Sachdev and Tillotson 2002](https://archive.org/details/buildingjaipurma0000sach); [Wikipedia, Vastu shastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vastu_shastra)). No scholarly study was found that dates the mirror rule, the usual five-element chart or the fixed north–south seat. The text-literate voices that could be read point the same way as this research: S. K. Ramachandra Rao in 1995 calls the classical room prescriptions "by no means uniform", and D. N. Shukla's 1960 survey finds a bath-house and a refuse ground in the texts and nothing on attached bathrooms [RD] ([Vasudev (ed.) 1998, pp. 23–25](https://archive.org/details/vastu-astrology-and-architecture-gayatri-devi-vasudev); [Shukla 1960](https://archive.org/details/irhd_vastu-shastra-vol.-1-hindu-science-of-architecture-by-d.-n.-shukla-1960-lucknow-)).

## Conclusion

The useful test for any bathroom rule is three questions: which text, which verse, and is the verse about a room or about a compound? Put to the classical corpus, they return a firm answer one level above this bathroom and silence inside it. That silence is historical, not accidental. The texts were written for compounds with tanks, wells and detached outbuildings, in a world where the householder walked out to relieve himself; the fixed seat in an attached bathroom reached ordinary Indian houses in the twentieth century, and the fixture rules took shape in English print only from the 1990s, most of them around 2000. Following those rules is a matter of family custom and of comfort with a consultant. It is not fidelity to śāstra, and declining them breaks no text found.

For this house that leaves two checks and one freedom. Confirm north on site and the room's measured position before any zone is quoted to or by a consultant, since two feet or eight degrees changes the answer, and look at the central block of the whole plan on a measured drawing. Then build the layout the designer prefers on practical grounds: Sketch B already carries the two features with a classical echo, a north-facing seat in a south-western room, and its north-wall mirror meets the modern custom halfway at no cost. What would change this report is narrow: a late regional manual with fixture rules, which the searches cannot exclude, or the unread manuals of 1987–1999 and Chakrabarti's study, which would refine the dating of the modern rules and are unlikely to give them a Sanskrit source.
